  We finally have everything we need to define our quantum pseudocode. 
    Our goal is to construct a well-defined pseudocode rich enough 
    to draw interaction diagrams like the one in \cref{fig:aliceandbob},
    with local operations agnostic to the global state of the
    system and any existing entanglement between its parts.
    Rather than quantum states, the central objects of our pseudocode will be
    \emph{qubit registers}, which is nothing but an ordered list of qubits,
    in analogy to the bit registers of classical computation.

  \subsubsection{Qubit Registers.}
    Let $\qubit$ be a physical qubit, in the sense of being some kind of
      particle or quantum system existing in
      the physical world. Now, guided by our intuition, we want to say that Alice,
      instead of sending a quantum \emph{state} to Bob, actually sends him the
      \emph{physical qubit} $\qubit$.\footnote{
        In real-world quantum technologies, qubits are
        typically stored in one kind of system (e.g.\ atoms) and sent using another
        kind (typically photons), but due to the non-cloneability of quantum states,
        together with the universal ability to transfer any quantum state from
        one qubit to another (via the $\SWAPgate$ gate), our simplified
        model is without loss of generality.
      } Anyone who receives a qubit, should also gain the
      ability to apply any transformation or measurement on it, while those who sent it 
      should lose the ability to do so, unless and until the qubit is returned to them.
      Crucially, we should be able to describe these actions \emph{locally},
      i.e.\ without reference to any qubits \emph{not} in the possession of the
      receiving party.

    So, with this goal in mind, let us introduce a little more formalism.

    \begin{definition}[Qubit]\label{def:qubit}
      A qubit $\qubit$ is a two-dimensional physical system, i.e.\ a physical
      system associated with an \emph{observable} $\hat{\qubit}$, taking values
      $\{m_0,m_1\}$ which can be mapped to $\bin$. Each qubit is additionally
      associated with 
      a unique \emph{register address} $i \in \NN$.\footnote{
        In terms of the original formulation of quantum Turing machines given by
        Deutsch~\cite{Deutsch85}, the address $i$ defined here corresponds
        to values of the \emph{address observable} $\hat{x}$
        (except that we limit ourselves to finite quantum memory).
      }     
    \end{definition}

    \noindent
    Let us refer to the qubit sitting at address $i$ as $\qubit_i$.
      If $\qubit_i$ is not entangled with any
      other qubit (i.e.\ it is in a pure state), then its state may be denoted
      $\ket{\qubit_i}$. If
      $\ket{\qubit_i} = \ket{\psi}$ for some particular single-qubit
      state $\ket{\psi}$, then this may be denoted $\ket{\psi}_i$. 

    Likewise,
      the state of qubit $i$ at time $t$ may be denoted $\ket{\qubit_i(t)}$.
      For the sake of illustration, say 
      the single-qubit unitary $\unitary$ was applied to $\qubit_i$ at time
      $t$; this would then be written 
      \[
        \ket{\qubit_i(t)} \apply{\unitary_i} \ket{\qubit_i(t+1)} \left( =
        \unitary_i \ket{\qubit_i(t)} \right)\, ,
      \]
      (and if $\qubit$ was initialized to $\ket0$, then $\ket{\qubit(0)} = \ket0$).

    Next, let us define our ``universe'', that is, the full, undivided quantum memory 
      which will act as a stage for all our plays.

    \begin{definition}[Quantum Memory]\label{def:memory}
      An $n$-qubit quantum \emph{memory} is an $n$-element list of qubits $\qcomp =
      (\qubit_1, \cdots, \qubit_n)$ comprising a quantum
      system that is \emph{closed} in the following sense: 
      There are no operations that may be performed on the available qubits $\qubit_i$
      such that $\qcomp$ is no longer in a pure state.
    \end{definition}

    \noindent
    In particular, $\qcomp$ can not get entangled with any
      system outside of itself---though it may be \emph{observed}, after which
      it will immediately collapse to a new pure state.\footnote{
        Of course, from the Everettian point of view, observing a state
        \emph{is} merely to entangle with it.
      }

    Thus, our $N$-dimensional ``global'' quantum state $\ket{\qcomp}$ will always
      be well-defined, giving us a ``smallest'' element $\qubit$ and a
      ``largest'' element $\qcomp$. Then it is time to define the in-betweens.

    \begin{definition}[Quantum Register]\label{def:register}
      An $m$-qubit quantum register $\register$ is a subsystem of the $n$-qubit quantum
      memory $\qcomp$, that is, an ordered sub-list of $\qcomp$, where by
      \emph{ordered} we mean that if
      $\register = (\qubit_{i_0}, \qubit_{i_1}, \dots, \qubit_{i_{m-1}})$
      then $i_0 < i_1 < \dots < i_{m-1}$.
    \end{definition}

    \noindent
    If the qubits in $\register$ are not entangled with any other subsystems
      of $\qcomp$, then the system $\register$ is in a pure state, and we may
      write $\ket{\register}$. In a slight abuse of notation, let
      $[\register]$ denote the set $\{i_j\}_{j\in [m]}$ of qubit addresses
      associated with the qubits in $\register$; then $\ket\register$ is given
      by
    \[ 
      \ket\register 
      \coloneqq \bigotimes_{\forall i \in [m]} \ket{\register[R][i]}
      \coloneqq \bigotimes_{\forall i \in [\register]} \ket{\qcomp[i]}
      = \ket{\qubit_{i_0}, \ldots, \qubit_{i_{m-1}}}\, .
    \]

    \noindent
    If $\ket{\register} = \ket{\psi}$ for some specific state $\ket{\psi}$,
      this may then be denoted $\ket{\psi}_{[\register]}$,
      which, having satisfied ourselves that it is
      well-defined, we will immediately replace with the shorthand $\ket{\psi}_{\register}$.

    But of course, the starting point of this entire exercise was that we
      can \emph{not} in general write 
      the quantum state of a qubit register as a pure state, and so we
      still need an alternative way to denote local operations on it.

  \subsubsection{Sending Qubits.}
    First, Alice and Bob should be able to send qubit registers to each
      other. Let $\register[A]$ and $\register[B]$ be the registers of qubits available to
      Alice and Bob, respectively. Then 
      ``Alice sends $\register$ to Bob'' can be expressed succinctly as
      \[
        \register[A] \apply{\register[R]} \register[B]\, ,
      \]
      which removes the subregister $\register[R]$ from $\register[A]$ and adds
      it to $\register[B]$. 
      %(See \cref{subsec:sending} for a formal
      %definition.)

  \subsubsection{Operating on Registers.}
    If our (global) quantum memory is $\qcomp$, and we apply (a circuit
      that implements the unitary) $\unitary$ onto register $\register$, we
      will write this
      \begin{align*}
        \applyU{\unitary}{\register}[\qcomp] &: \ket{\qcomp} \longrightarrow
          \unitary_{\register} \ket{\qcomp}\, .
      \end{align*}

    \noindent
    With this notation, it now becomes clear that although Alice only operates on
      the qubits that are available to her, those very same operations may
      affect distant parts of the global state $\ket\qcomp$ through entanglement. 
      Whenever the global state $\qcomp$ is clear
      from context, we will drop the subscript and
      simply write $\applyU{\unitary}{\register}$. 

  \subsubsection{Measuring Registers.}
    Finally, we need to measure the qubits at some point and read out the
      classical information. Given a projective measurement operator
      $\measurement$ and a register $\register$, we write the act of
      measuring $\register$ according to $\measurement$ as
      \[
        x \sample \meas{\measurement}{\register}[\qcomp]\, .
      \]
      Note that $x$ is a classical \emph{sampled} value, as it must be, since
      measurements are inherently stochastic. 
        
    The post-measurement will be 
      \[
        \ket{\qcomp} \apply{\measurement} \ket{\qcomp}_{\Sspace_i}\, ,
      \]
      where $\ket{\qcomp}_{\Sspace_i}$ is the result of projecting the
      qubits of $\register$ onto subspace $\Sspace_i$, and applying the
      identity to the rest.

    As before, we omit the subscript whenever $\qcomp$ is clear from context,
      and we also omit the measurement operator $\measurement$ whenever we are
      performing a computational-basis measurement; for example, measuring
      $\ell$ qubits and observing the bit string $x \in \bin^\ell$ will
      be written simply
    \[
      x \sample \meas{}{\register}\, .
    \]

    \noindent
    The above notation should hopefully be intuitively clear, though coming up
      with a precise definitions takes a little extra work; you can find this in
      \cref{sec:pseudocode}. 

    To showcase the utility of our pseudocode, we can now restate the Born
      rule in a very compact form.

    \begin{lemma}[The Born Rule]\label{def:Born}
      If qubit register $\register$ is in pure state $\ket{\register} =
      \sum_i \alpha_i \ket{x_i}$, then
      \[
        \probsample{x \sample \meas{}{\register}}{x = x_i} = \abs{\alpha_i}^2\, .
      \]
    \end{lemma}
