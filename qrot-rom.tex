  \subsection{One-way PKE to Hiding KEM: The $\U$-transform}
    The Fujisaki-Okamoto transformation~\cite{C:FujOka99}, which underlies many post-quantum
      KEMs including the now-standardized ML-KEM (Kyber)~\cite{Kyber}, can be
      framed as the composition of two 
      transformations, \T and \U, as defined by Hofheinz
      et al.~\cite{TCC:HofHovKil17} (hereafter HHK). 
      Starting from a public-key
      encryption scheme, the first transformation (\T) derandomizes the
      PKE by having its randomness be derived from a hash of the message (and possibly
      the public key), and additionally adds a re-encryption check to the
      decryption algorithm, while the second transformation builds a KEM from a
      deterministic PKE by sampling a uniform message, encrypting it, and
      hashing it to produce the ephemeral key $\eK$.
  
    In the following, we will focus on this second transformation, $\U$,
      and build our KEM that satisfies indistinguishability (sometimes known as
      ``hiding''), starting with a deterministing PKE that satisfies
      one-way security.\footnote{
        HHK defined several variants of the \U-transform that differ from the variant discussed here
    		in how they handle invalid ciphertexts and in what they hash into the
        output key; our variant corresponds to \UExpMess in their notation.
        %\hhnote{
        %  Should we do the more general \UExpMess transform instead? Or does that
        %  just muddy the message?
        %}
      } (See App.~\ref{sec:ttransform} for details on the \T-transform.)

    \hhnote{
      Leave this version without the extra re-encryption check (which also
      matches the original \U), and define a new \U with the re-encryption check
      when we later want CCA security.
    }

    \noindent
    After the \U-transform, if the original $\dPKE$ satisfied a \emph{minimal}
      security requirement, namely that given a ciphertext, it should be hard to
      derive the encrypted plaintext, then $\KEM$ achieves the much stronger
      notion that given an encapsulation of one of two possible symmetric keys,
      you should not be able to figure out which of the two keys sits inside the
      encapsulation. In other words, if $\dPKE$ satisfies one-wayness
      (\autoref{def:ow}), then $\KEM$ satisfies indistinguishability
      (\autoref{def:indcpa}), captured in the following theorem.

    Intuitively, the reduction relies on the fact that in order to
      distinguish, an adversary will have to call the random oracle on one of
      the messages used to derive the ephemeral key. Since the reduction 
      completely controls the random oracle simulation, it can simply output the
      correct solution when it is used in a query to the random oracle. The fact that $\dPKE$
      is deterministic means that our reduction will immediately be able to
      recognize it, and this is why we are able to get a tight bound in this
      case.

    \begin{theorem}[$\dPKE$ is OW $\implies$ $\KEM$ is IND]\label{thm:cparom}
      Let $\dPKE$ be a deterministic PKE, let $\RO$ be a random oracle, 
      and let $\KEM = \U[\PKE, \RO]$.
      Then for every adversary $\adv$ against $\KEM$, 
      %that makes at most $\qe$ calls to the challenge oracle, 
      there is a reduction $\bdv$ against $\dPKE$ such that
      \[
        \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} \leq \advantage{\owcpa}{\dPKE}[(\bdv)]\, .
        %+ \frac{\qe}{2^m}\, .
      \]
    \end{theorem}

    \noindent
    \paragraph{Parsing the statement.}
      If you are new to security reductions, it may be hard to see at a glance
        how the theorem statement connects to the intuition that ``if
        $\dPKE$ achieves one-wayness security, then $\KEM$ achieves distinguishing
        security'', so let us take a moment to parse the different parts of the
        statement.

      On the left hand side, we have the advantage of our adversary $\adv$.
        The theorem quantifies over all possible $\adv$, so you may
        think of this as the maximum possible distinguishing advantage against
        $\KEM$. On the right, we have an upper-bound on this largest-possible advantage,
        so if the right-hand side is small, then the largest-possible
        distinguishing advantage against $\KEM$ must also be small, which is
        just a different way of saying that $\KEM$ is secure.

      The term on the right-hand side is the one-wayness advantage of our
        \emph{reduction} $\bdv$. The quantifiers tell us that for every $\adv$,
        there exists a $\bdv$ (which may depend on $\adv$) that achieves this
        advantage. The main challenge of the
        proof is then to construct a $\bdv$ that exploits the winning
        strategy of $\adv$ to win at its own game.

      The trick to understand how this proves security is to think
        contrapositively. Recall that what we
        want to prove is that ``if $\dPKE$ is secure, then $\KEM$ is secure''.
        The conrapositive statement that we are actually proving is ``if $\KEM$
        is \emph{in}secure, then $\dPKE$ must \emph{also} be insecure''. 
        %Combined with the starting assumption that $\dPKE$
        %is secure, this will then give us a proof-by-contradiction that $\KEM$
        %must also be secure. 
        Hence, instead of thinking of $\bdv$'s advantage as
        an upper-bound on the best-possible $\adv$ advantage, think of this
        best-possible $\adv$ advantage as a \emph{lower-bound} on the
        advantage an adversary $\bdv$ could achieve against $\dPKE$: If the former is large,
        then the latter must also be large.
        %(at least as long as the second term is small by comparison). 
        %
        Since we started out assuming that
        $\dPKE$ is secure, which is to say that the one-way advantage of \emph{any} $\bdv$
        must be small, this gives us our proof-by-contradiction.

      %The second term is an artifact of our
      %  proof strategy. (We could have come up with a slightly smarter
      %  reduction that avoids it, see Exercise~\ref{ex:remove-loss-term}.) 
      %  We still have to ensure that this term is small for the bound
      %  to be meaningful, in particular much smaller than $1$. 
      %  The variable $\qe$ counts the number of challenges, that is, the number
      %  of KEM encapsulations that the adversary sees. In realistic settings,
      %  a single public key may issue a large number of encapsulations, so let's
      %  be conservative and assume that $\qe \leq 2^{30} \approx 1$ billion. 
      %  The denominator $2^m$ is the size of the output space of the random
      %  oracle. Recall that the output of the random oracle will be used as a
      %  symmetric key $K$, and these are typically bitstrings of length $128$.
      %  If so, then the second term is upper-bounded by

    \paragraph{Our proof strategy.}
      Our reduction will not be able simulate the real world of the
        indistinguishability game, since it does not know which message the
        challenge ciphertext encrypted, and so it can not hash it to produce the
        correct symmetric key for $\adv$.
        It can, however, easily simulate the random world, since here the
        symmetric key is simply sampled uniformly.  

      But in order to argue that $\adv$ can not distinguish this from the real
        world, we need to change the game in such a way that the move from real to
        random keys is completely hidden from view.
        We do this by inserting an intermediary game between the real and the
        random worlds, as shown in \autoref{fig:cparom-hops}.
        This middle game formalizes the idea that, thanks to the \U-transform,
        the adversary will have to call the random oracle on the correct message
        in order to notice that it is not in the real-world experiment: If it
        does, then a $\bad$ flag is set to true; other than this, the game
        proceeds exactly as the real-world game. Thanks to
        \autoref{lemma:bad}, we know that the difference between these games is
        bounded by the probability that $\bad$ is set to true.

      Now, \emph{conditioned} on $\bad$ never being set to true,
        notice that we can exchange the real symmetric keys for a randomly
        sampled one without the adversary ever being able to notice. 
        Therefore, the second game hop from real to random keys is ``for free'',
        since the two games are equal in terms of probabilities. 

      We now have a game that $\bdv$ knows how to simulate. Further, the $\bad$
        flag was chosen in such a way that the case that it is set to $\true$
        corresponds exactly to the case that $\bdv$ finds the value that it is
        looking for and wins the one-wayness game. 
        In other words: \emph{either} $\adv$ never calls the
        random oracle on one of the correct queries, in which case its view is
        perfectly consistent with being in the real-world game, \emph{or} $\bdv$
        wins. Pseudocode for $\bdv$ is given in \autoref{fig:cparom-red}.

      Finally, we remove the check for a $\bad$ event again, which gives us a
        game that is exactly equivalent to the random-world $\indcpa$ game. 
        Since we are already conditioning on $\bad$ being false, this change is
        also for free.
        
      To summarize: In terms of probabilities, the difference between 
        $\game 0$ and $\game 1$ is the probability that $\bad$ is ever set to
        $\true$, the difference between $\game 1$ and $\game 2$ is zero.
        $\bdv$ simulates $\game 2$, employing a strategy such that its
        one-wayness advantage is exactly
        the probability that $\bad$ would be set to $\true$. 
        Finally, the difference between $\game 2$ and $\game 3$ is zero
        conditioned on $\neg \bad$, the latter
        being equal to the random-world game.

    \begin{proof}[\autoref{thm:cparom}]
      \begin{align*}
        \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} 
          &= \abs{
            \condprob{\experiment{\indcpa}{\KEM}}{b = 0}
            - \condprob{\experiment{\indcpa}{\KEM}}{b = 1}
          } \\
          &= \abs{
            \prob{\game{0}(\adv)} - \prob{\game{3}(\adv)}
          } \\
          &= \abs{
            \prob{\game{0}(\adv)} - \prob{\game{1}(\adv)}
            + \prob{\game{1}(\adv)} - \prob{\game{2}(\adv)} 
            + \prob{\game{2}(\adv)} - \prob{\game{3}(\adv)} 
          } \\
          &\leq \abs{\prob{\game{0}(\adv)} - \prob{\game{1}(\adv)}}
            + \abs{\prob{\game{1}(\adv)} - \prob{\game{2}(\adv)}}
            + \abs{\prob{\game{2}(\adv)} - \prob{\game{3}(\adv)}} \\
          &\leq \prob{\bad} + 
            \abs{
              \prob{\game{0}(\adv)} - \condprob{\game{1}(\adv)}{\neg \bad}
            } \\
          &\quad + \abs{
            \condprob{\game{1}(\adv)}{\neg \bad} - \condprob{\game{2}(\adv)}{\neg \bad}
          } \\
          &\quad + \abs{
            \condprob{\game{2}(\adv)}{\neg \bad} - \prob{\game{3}(\adv)}
          } \\
          &= \prob{\bad} + 0 + 0 + 0 \\
          &= \prob{\bad} = \prob{\experiment{\owcpa}{\dPKE}(\bdv)} =
          \advantage{\owcpa}{\dPKE}(\bdv)\, .
      \end{align*}
    \end{proof}

    \paragraph{One-way to hiding.}
      In order to (\ldots)
        a general ``one-way to hiding lemma''. Since the reduction is so
        simple, this may seem to only add an unnecessary level of formalism.
        This same formalism will however turn out to be one of the most useful
        tools we have when arguing about the quantum case.

      \begin{lemma}[Classical O2H, Find]\label{thm:co2h-find}
        let $\RO: \bin^n \to \bin^m$ be a random
        oracle, and let $z$ be a bitstring that depends arbitrarily on $\RO$.
        Let $\solset \subseteq \bin^n$ be a subset of inputs, and let $\RO
        \backslash \solset$ be the punctured random oracle that is equal to $\RO$
        on all inputs $x \not\in \solset$, but which outputs $\bot$ on all points
        in $\solset$. Let $\find$ be the event that the random oracle ever outputs
        $\bot$. Then for every distinguisher $\distinguisher$,
        \[
            \abs{\prob{1 \sample \distinguisher^{\ro}(z)}
                - \prob{1 \sample \distinguisher^{\ro\backslash \solset}(z)}}
              = \probsample{\distinguisher^{\ro \backslash \solset}(z)}{\find}\, .
        \]
      \end{lemma}
