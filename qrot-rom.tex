\section{The Random Oracle Model}\label{sec:rom}
  Random oracles are nice. 

  \subsection{Simulating Random Oracles}
    They are also very easy to simulate, a process known as
      lazy-sampling, as shown in \autoref{fig:lazy-sampling}.

  \subsection{One-way PKE to Hiding KEM: The $\U$-transform}
    The Fujisaki-Okamoto transformation~\cite{C:FujOka99}, which underlies many post-quantum
      KEMs including the now-standardized ML-KEM (Kyber)~\cite{Kyber}, can be
      framed as the composition of two 
      transformations, \T and \U~\cite{TCC:HofHovKil17}.
      %, as defined by Hofheinz et al. (hereafter HHK). 
      Starting from a public-key
      encryption scheme, the first transformation \T derandomizes the
      PKE by having its randomness be derived from a hash of the message (and possibly
      the public key), and additionally adds a re-encryption check to the
      decryption algorithm, while the second transformation \U builds a KEM from a
      deterministic PKE by sampling a uniform message, encrypting it, and
      hashing it to produce the symmetric key $\eK$.
  
    \hhnote{
      Leave this version without the extra re-encryption check (which also
      matches the original \U), and define a new \U with the re-encryption check
      when we later want CCA security.
    }

      we associate the KEM $\KEM \coloneqq \U[\PKE, \ROkey]$ defined in \autoref{fig:def:U-transform}.

    \noindent
    We will use the \U-transformation as our running example,
      using it to build a KEM that satisfies indistinguishability
      from a deterministing PKE that satisfies
      one-wayness.\footnote{
        When Hofheinz et al.\ first modularized the FO transform into
        its \T and \U components, they defined several variants of the
        \U-transform that differ from the variant discussed here
    		in how they handle invalid ciphertexts and in what they hash into the
        output key; our variant corresponds to \UExpMess in their notation.~\cite{TCC:HofHovKil17}
        %\hhnote{
        %  Should we do the more general \UExpMess transform instead? Or does that
        %  just muddy the message?
        %}
      } (Details on the \T-transform are given in App.~\ref{sec:ttransform}.)

    As explained in \autoref{sec:basics}, One-way security under Chosen
      Plaintext Attacks, or \owcpa, is really a minimal security
      requirement for PKE schemes: The goal of One-Wayness (OW) simply says that
      given a ciphertext, it should be hard to recover the plaintext, and the
      power of Chosen Plaintext Attacks (CPA), the ability to encrypt any
      message under the key you are targetting, are of course ever-present when
      encryption is public.
      What we want is the much stronger security requirement of
      \emph{indistinguishability}, that even when two candidate messages are
      known, an adversary is not able to figure out which of the two is
      encrypted. Since our public key scheme is deterministic, it actually
      \emph{cannot} achieve this notion of security: With two candidate messages
      known, an adversary can simply re-encrypt both messages and check which of
      the two yields the challenge ciphertext!
      And yet, taking the deterministic PKE scheme through the \U-transform
      will give us a \KEM that \emph{does} achieve indistinguishability.

    The central object that allows us to cross the wide chasm
      between one-wayness and indistinguishability, is precisely the
      \emph{random oracle}.
      Intuitively, the security reduction relies on the fact that in order to
      distinguish, an adversary will have to call the random oracle on one of
      the messages used to derive the ephemeral key. Since we
      fully control the simulation of the random oracle, we can simply output the
      correct solution when it is used in a query to the random oracle. The fact
      that $\dPKE$ is deterministic means that our reduction will immediately be
      able to recognize the correct solution; this fact allows us to get
      a tight bound in this case. (Otherwise we would have to guess which of the
      calls to the random oracle contained the solution we are looking for,
      which would incur a tightness loss related to the probability that
      we guess correctly.)

    To summarize, if $\dPKE$ satisfies one-wayness
      (\autoref{def:ow}), then $\KEM$ satisfies indistinguishability
      (\autoref{def:indcpa}), captured in the following theorem.

    \hhnote{
      Defs for OW-CPA and IND-CPA still todo in \autoref{sec:basics}.
    }

    \begin{theorem}[$\dPKE$ is OW $\implies$ $\KEM$ is IND]
      \label{thm:cparom}
      Let \dPKE be a deterministic PKE scheme, let \RO be a random oracle, 
      and let $\KEM$ be the KEM given by $\U[\dPKE, \RO]$.
      Then for every adversary $\adv$ against $\KEM$, 
      %that makes at most $\qe$ calls to the challenge oracle, 
      there is an adversary $\bdv$ against $\dPKE$ such that
      \[
        \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} \leq \advantage{\owcpa}{\dPKE}[(\bdv)]\, .
        %+ \frac{\qe}{2^m}\, .
      \]
      The adversary $\bdv$ runs in the same time as $\adv$, plus the %(relatively small) 
      overhead of lazy-sampling a random oracle.
    \end{theorem}

    \noindent
    \paragraph{Parsing the Statement.}
      If you are new to security reductions, it may be hard to see at a glance
        how the theorem statement connects to the intuition that ``if
        $\dPKE$ achieves one-wayness security, then $\KEM$ achieves distinguishing
        security'', so let us take a moment to parse the different parts of the
        statement.

      On the left-hand side of the main inequality, we have the distinguishing
        advantage of our given adversary $\adv$.
        The theorem quantifies over all possible $\adv$, so you may
        think of this as the maximum possible distinguishing advantage against
        the construction. On the right-hand side, we have an upper-bound on this
        largest-possible distinguishing advantage. Hence,
        if the right-hand side is small, then the largest-possible
        distinguishing advantage against $\KEM$ must also be small. This is
        just a different way of saying that $\KEM$ is indistinguishable.
        The term on the right-hand side is the one-wayness advantage of our
        \emph{constructed} adversary $\bdv$. Note the quantifiers: the theorem
        statement says that ``for every (given) $\adv$, there exists a
        (constructed) $\bdv$''. Hence, while we have no way of knowing the inner
        workings of the given adversary $\adv$, we are free to construct $\bdv$ to our
        liking. 

      The trick to understand how this ``proves security'' is to think
        contrapositively. Recall that what we
        want to prove is that ``if $\dPKE$ is secure, then $\KEM$ is secure''.
        The conrapositive statement that we are actually proving is ``if $\KEM$
        is \emph{in}secure, then $\dPKE$ must \emph{also} be insecure''. 
        Hence, instead of thinking of $\bdv$'s advantage as
        an upper-bound on the best-possible $\adv$ advantage, think of this
        best-possible $\adv$ advantage as a \emph{lower-bound} on the
        advantage an adversary $\bdv$ could achieve against $\dPKE$: If the
        former is large (\KEM is broken),
        then the latter must also be large (\dPKE is broken).
        Since we started out assuming that $\dPKE$ is secure, 
        %which is to say that the one-way advantage of \emph{any} $\bdv$ must be small, 
        this gives us our proof-by-contradiction.

      The main challenge of the
        proof is then to construct a $\bdv$ that exploits the winning
        strategy of $\adv$ in such a way that if $\adv$ \emph{would} win at the
        indistinguishability game, then what \emph{actually} happens is that
        $\bdv$ wins at the one-wayness game (at least with high probability).

    \begin{proof} %[\autoref{thm:cparom}]
      Our constructed adversary $\bdv$ will not be able simulate the real world of the
        indistinguishability game, since it does not know which message the
        challenge ciphertext it recieved encrypted (that's what it's trying to
        figure out); hence, it can not hash the message to produce the
        correct symmetric key for $\adv$.
        It can, however, easily simulate the random world, since here the
        symmetric key is simply sampled uniformly.  

      But in order to argue that $\adv$ can not distinguish this from the real
        world, we need to change the game in such a way that the move from real to
        random keys is completely hidden from view.
        We do this by inserting two intermediary games between the real-world and the
        random-world games, as shown in \autoref{fig:cparom-hops}.
        These middle games formalize the idea that, thanks to the \U-transform,
        the adversary will have to call the random oracle on the correct message
        in order to notice that it is not in the real world: If it
        does, then a $\bad$ flag is set to true. Assuming the $\bad$ flag is
        never set to true, the game proceeds exactly as the real-world game, and
        thanks to \autoref{lemma:bad}, we know that the difference between these
        games is bounded by the probability that $\bad$ is set to true.
        Then, \emph{conditioned} on $\bad$ never being set to true,
        we can exchange the real symmetric key for a randomly
        sampled one without the adversary ever being able to notice. 
        %Therefore, the second game hop from real to random keys is ``for free'',
        %since the two games are equal in terms of probabilities.

      To make this formal, start by recalling the distinguishing advantage of
        the given adversary $\adv$. We will use the ``difference'' version of
        distinguishing advantages, as
        it fits more cleanly with the ``game hopping'' style of proof.
        \[
        	\advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} = \abs{
        		\condprobsample{
        			\experiment{\indcpa}{\KEM}
        		}{1 \gets \adv}{\chalbit = 0}
        		- \condprobsample{
        			\experiment{\indcpa}{\KEM}
        		}{1 \gets \adv}{\chalbit = 1}
        	}\, .
        \]
        The game $\game 1$ is just the real-world ($\chalbit=0$) $\indcpa$ game, with the
        construction of \KEM written out explicitly, together with a lazy-sampled
        random oracle. Similarly, $\game 3$ is just the random-world
        ($\chalbit=1$) $\indcpa$ game with the \KEM construction explicit. We
        can therefore write
        \[
        	\advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} = \abs{
        		\probsample{ \game{1}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv}
        		- \probsample{ \game{4}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv}
        	}\, .
        \]
        We are of course always free to ``add zero'' by adding and subtracting
        the same term, so this is equal to:
        \begin{align*}
          \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} &= |\probsample{ \game{1}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv} \\
            &\qquad - \probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv} + \probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv} \\
            &\qquad - \probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv} + \probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv} \\
            &\qquad - \probsample{ \game{4}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv}|\, .
        \end{align*}
        The ``fundamental lemma of game hopping'' is simply the idea that we can  
        now group terms and apply the triangle inequality to get $n-1$
        distinguishing advantages (or ``hops'') across $n$ games:
        \begin{align*}
          \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} &\leq 
            \abs{\probsample{ \game{1}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv} 
              - \probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv}} \\
            &\qquad + \abs{\probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv} 
              - \probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv}} \\
            &\qquad + \abs{\probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv}
              - \probsample{ \game{4}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv}}\, .
        \end{align*}
        In this case, the only difference between $\game 1$ and $\game 2$ is 
        introduction of a $\bad$ flag, which is completely hidden from view from
        the adversary and therefore cannot affect its behaviour. As a result, the
        first difference evaluates to $0$. Since the same is true for 
        $\game 3$ vs.\ $\game 4$, we actually have:
        \begin{align*}
          \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} &\leq 
            \abs{\probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv} 
              - \probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv}}\, . 
        \end{align*}
        Let us use the law of total probability (and the triangle inequality
        again) to split into two distinguishing advantages, one for
        the case that $\bad$ is set to $\true$ and one for the case that $\bad$
        remains $\false$.
        \begin{align*}
          \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} &\leq 
            \abs{ \probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv \land \neg \bad} 
              - \probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv \land \neg \bad}} \\
            &\qquad + \abs{ \probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv \land \bad} 
              - \probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv \land \bad}}\, .
        \end{align*}
        These distinguishing advantages represent the probability that the adversary notices
        that we are exchanging the real key for a random key. Notice that
        $\dPKE$ being deterministic and correct imposes a one-to-one
        correspondence between messages and ciphertexts. This means that if $\bad$ 
        is never set to $\true$, then re-encryption never yielded $\chalctxt$,
        which in turn implies that the random oracle was never called on $x^*$. 
        Thanks to the uniform nature of random oracles, this means that 
        both real and random $\eK^*$ will look completely random from the
        point of view of $\adv$. Hence, the two probabilities 
        $\probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv \land \neg \bad}$
        and
        $\probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv \land \neg \bad}$
        are equal, and the $\neg \bad$ part of the distinguishing advantage is
        zero:
        \begin{align*}
          \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} &\leq 
            \abs{ \probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv \land \bad} 
              - \probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{1 \gets \adv \land \bad}}\, . 
        \end{align*}
        We have reached the crux of the matter: The distinguishing
        advantage of $\adv$ can be characterized as the difference in its
        probability of outputting $1$ \emph{and} in calling the random oracle on
        the correct $x^*$. But notice that 1) up until the point that $\bad$ is set to
        $\true$, $\eK^*$ will look completely random to $\adv$, and 2) \emph{after}
        $\bad$ is set to $\true$, an adversary would easily be able to
        distinguish, e.g.\ by using $\dPKE$ to
        re-encrypt $x^*$ and confirm that it gives the correct
        $\chalctxt$, then comparing $\eK^*$ against $\RO(x^*)$.
        We therefore lose nothing of significance if we simply bound this
        distinguishing advantage by the probability that $\bad$ is set to $\true$.
        \begin{align*}
          \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} &\leq 
            \probsample{ \game{2}(\adv) }{\bad} 
              = \probsample{ \game{3}(\adv) }{\bad}\, ,
        \end{align*}
        where, again, the latter equality holds due to the view of $\adv$ being equal
        in both games up until the point that $\bad$ is set to $\true$, since
        random oracles are perfectly preimage-hiding.

      We now have a bound in terms of $\game 3$ only, which is a game that a
        one-wayness adversary $\bdv$ can simulate while injecting its own
        challenge ciphertext into the simulation. 
        Further, the $\bad$ flag was chosen in such a way that the case that
        $\bad$ is set to $\true$ corresponds exactly to the case that $\bdv$
        finds the challenge message $x^*$ required to win the one-wayness game.
        Since the quantifiers in the theorem statement only requires that for
        every $\adv$, there \emph{exists} a $\bdv$ that achieves the stated
        bound, we are free to construct such a $\bdv$ explicitly;
        one solution is shown in pseudocode in \autoref{fig:cparom-red}.

      To summarize: \emph{Either} $\adv$ never calls the
        random oracle on the correct $x^*$ ($\bad = \false$), 
        in which case the view of $\adv$ is
        perfectly consistent with being in the real-world game, \emph{or else} it
        called $\RO$ on $x^*$ ($\bad = \true$), in which case our constructed
        adversary $\bdv$ given in \autoref{fig:cparom-red} wins at the one-wayness
        game. This allows us to conclude that, regardless of the attack strategy
        employed by the given adversary $\adv$, our constructed adversary $\bdv$
        wins the one-wayness game with advantage
      \[
        \advantage{\owcpa}{\dPKE}[(\bdv)] =
          \probsample{\game{3}(\adv)}{\bad}\, ,
      \]
      so that
      \[
        \advantage{\indcpa}{\KEM} \leq 
        \advantage{\owcpa}{\dPKE}[(\bdv)]\, . 
      \]
      \qed
    \end{proof}

      \subsubsection{One-way to Hiding.}
        In order to (\ldots)
          a general ``one-way to hiding lemma''. Since the reduction is so
          simple, this may seem to only add an unnecessary level of formalism.
          This same formalism will however turn out to be one of the most useful
          tools we have when arguing about the quantum case.

        \begin{lemma}[Classical O2H, Find]\label{thm:co2h-find}
          let $\RO: \bin^n \to \bin^m$ be a random
          oracle, and let $z$ be a bitstring that depends arbitrarily on $\RO$.
          Let $\solset \subseteq \bin^n$ be a subset of inputs, and let $\RO
          \backslash \solset$ be the punctured random oracle that is equal to $\RO$
          on all inputs $x \not\in \solset$, but which outputs $\bot$ on all points
          in $\solset$. Let $\find$ be the event that the random oracle ever outputs
          $\bot$. Then for every distinguisher $\distinguisher$,
          \[
              \abs{\prob{1 \sample \distinguisher^{\ro}(z)}
                  - \prob{1 \sample \distinguisher^{\ro\backslash \solset}(z)}}
                = \probsample{\distinguisher^{\ro \backslash \solset}(z)}{\find}\, .
          \]
        \end{lemma}
