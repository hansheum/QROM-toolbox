  Proofs of security in the Random Oracle Model are often conceptually
    simple, and yet regularly achieve strong security claims with
    near-tightness for highly efficient constructions---which is as close as
    one gets to a holy trinity for provable security. In contrast, proofs in the
    Quantum Random Oracle Model are often hard to understand---even requiring
    specialized knowledge in quantum information---and infamously lossy.

  And yet, if we want our proofs of security to have merit in a post-quantum
    world, it seems that our choice is between the Standard Model and the
    QROM. This is because random oracles model hash functions, which are
    publicly available algorithms that anyone can run locally on their machine.
    Therefore, there is nothing stopping someone with a quantum computer to
    boot it up and run it on a superposition of inputs. If we want to
    accurately model quantum attackers, then our model must include this
    capability. 

  In the standard model, of course, this is not a problem at all,
    since then the hash functions in question is provided to the attacker
    as-is.  But if QROM proofs are lossy, then Standard Model constructions
    that achieve the same goals as their ROM counterparts are often highly
    inefficient in comparison---and, as every cryptographer knows, efficiency
    trumps security in real-world implementations.

  Therefore, as we inch ever closer to the looming threat of
    cryptographically-relevant quantum computers, it is important that the
    barrier for entry into the Quantum Random Oracle Model is lowered as much as
    possible, so that the community may together bring our transition to a
    post-quantum world into effect, in a safe and (provably) secure manner.

  \paragraph{The ROM techniques don't transfer \ldots}
    One of the most useful facts about reductions in the random oracle model,
      is that anyone can efficiently \emph{simulate} a random oracle, simply by
      drawing the random outputs one-at-a-time, and storing them in a list to
      ensure consistency. This technique, known as \emph{lazy sampling}, allows
      the reduction to not only implement the oracle efficiently, but also to
      see what input values the adversary is interested in, as well as
      adaptively \emph{reprogram} the oracle on inputs of its choice.
      Indeed, much of the strength of the random oracle model lies in exactly
      these capabilities.

    None of this transfers to the case where the oracle is quantum-accessible.
      To see why, consider an adversary that queries the oracle on a
      superposition of all possible inputs at once: The simulator would have to
      store an exponentially large list of input values in order just to process the
      input!

  \paragraph{\ldots But the concepts do.}
    And yet, the past decade has seen the development of techniques that
      recover much of these capabilities. Prime among these are the various
      One-Way to Hiding (O2H) lemmas, that allow for careful reprogramming of
      the quantum random oracle, as well as limited query extraction; and the
      Compressed Random Oracle (CRO) technique, which allows a reduction to
      efficiently implement a simulation of a quantum random oracle, in direct
      correspondence to the ``lazy sampling'' technique described above.

  \paragraph{Outline.}
    After defining our notation in \autoref{sec:prelim}, we define quantum
      random oracles in \autoref{sec:defs}. We provide several definitions, and
      show that they are all equivalent. In the process, we will try to get a
      ``feel'' for QROs, and we discuss how to (and how not to) reason
      intuitively about them.

    In \autoref{sec:o2h}, we
      introduce our main motivating scheme, a hybrid public-key encryption
      scheme due to Bellare and Rogaway~\cite{CCS:BelRog93}, and show that it
      achieves indistinguishability from the one-wayness of the underlying key
      encapsulation mechanism, by applying Unruh's One-Way to Hiding
      lemma~\cite{ACM:Unruh14}. We then generalize this
      result several times, through application of newer and more general
      variations of the lemma, including the Semi-Classical O2H lemma due to
      Ambainis et al.~\cite{C:AmbHamUnr19}, the Adaptive Resampling lemma of
      Grilo et al.~\cite{AC:GHHM21}, the Adaptive Reprogramming framework of
      Pan and Zeng~\cite{PKC:PanZen24}, and the Fixed-Permutation framework of
      Jaeger~\cite{EPRINT:Jaeger24b}. The goal of this exercise, of course, is
      \emph{not} to show that the Bellare--Rogaway encryption scheme is secure,
      but rather to gain an understanding of the various O2H lemmas that exist
      in the literature, and when and how to apply each.

    Then, in \autoref{sec:compressed}, we introduce the Compressed Random
      Oracle technique, which is to QROs as lazy sampling is to classical
      random oracles.
      This incredibly powerful technique takes some effort to wrap one's head
      around, and so we dedicate significant space to give gentle and
      intuitive explanations for all its moving parts, as they are introduced.

    Finally, with this technique in hand, we demonstrate its power by showing 
      in \autoref{sec:handsoff} how we can use it to prove statements that were
      previously beyond our reach.

    In addition, \autoref{sec:primer} contains a primer on quantum computing,
      including introductions to all the concepts and definitions for all the
      notation used in this text. It also includes several exercises to test
      your understanding, solutions to which are provided in \autoref{sec:solutions}.
