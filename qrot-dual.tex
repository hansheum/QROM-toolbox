  \subsection{Dual Alphabets}
    We introduce the dual-alphabet notation, which allows us to reason about qubit
      states in both the computational and the Hadamard basis without having to
      explicitly apply the Hadamard transformation every time.
      Recall the the Hadamard basis states: 
      \[
        \ket{+} \coloneqq \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\, ,\quad
        \ket{-} \coloneqq \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\, .
      \]
      For $b \in \{0,1\}$, define the state
      \[
        \ket{\hat{b}} \coloneqq \frac{\ket 0 + (-1)^b \ket 1}{\sqrt 2}\, ,
      \]
      i.e.\ $\ket{\hat{b}} = \ket +$ if $b=0$, and $\ket{\hat{b}} = \ket -$ if
      $b=1$. Notice how we are invited to think of the variable $\hat{b}$ as a bit
      defined over the alphabet $\{\hat 0, \hat 1 \} \coloneqq \{+,-\}$, in place
      of our usual alphabet $\{0,1\}$, since $\ket{\hat 0} = \ket +$ and 
      $\ket{\hat 1} = \ket -$. We will refer to this as the \emph{dual} alphabet.

    Going to multiple qubits, Hadamard-transforming an
      $n$-qubit computational-basis state $\ket{x} = \ket{101 \ldots 01}$ just 
      applies the Hadamard gate to each qubit individually, which gives us:
    \[
      \ket{x} = \ket{101 \ldots 01}
        \apply{\Hgate^{\otimes n}}\ket{- + - \ldots + -} 
        = \ket{\hat{1} \hat{0} \hat{1} \ldots \hat{0} \hat{1}} 
        \eqqcolon \ket{\hat{x}}\, .
    \]
    The result is a state labelled by a bitstring $\hat{x}$ defined over the
    dual alphabet: $\hat{x} \in \{\hat 0, \hat 1\}^n = \{+,-\}^n$.

    The computational basis and Hadamard basis really are
      \emph{dual} bases,\footnote{
        More precisely, they are \emph{mutually unbiased} bases.
      } and so are their alphabets: Writing out the state $\ket{\hat{x}}$ in
      terms of the computational basis, we have:
    \[
      \ket{\hat{x}} = 2^{-\nicefrac{n}{2}} \sum_{y} (-1)^{x \cdot
      y} \ket{y}\, ,
    \]
    which also holds the other way around:
    \[
      \ket{x} = 2^{-\nicefrac{n}{2}} \sum_{y} (-1)^{x \cdot
      y} \ket{\hat{y}}\, .
    \]
    In particular, for single-qubit states, 
    \[
      \ket{b} = \frac{\ket{\hat{0}} + (-1)^b \ket{\hat{1}}}{\sqrt 2}\, ,
      %= \frac{\ket{+} + (-1)^b \ket{-}}{\sqrt 2}\, ,
    \]
    which is the dual to our definition of $\ket{\hat{b}}$ above.

    Hence, while measuring a state $\ket x$ in the computational basis yields
      outcome $x$ with certainty, measuring a state $\ket{\hat{x}}$ in the
      computational basis yields a uniformly random outcome $x^\prime \sample \{0,1\}^n$;
      and while measuring a state $\ket{\hat{x}}$ in the Hadamard basis yields
      outcome $x$ with certainty, measuring a computational-basis state $\ket{x}$ in the
      Hadamard basis yields a uniformly random outcome $x^\prime \in \{0,1\}^n$.
      (Note how hats only ever show up in \emph{state labels},
      and are never used to denote the outcome; our classical bitstrings are still only
      defined over the alphabet $\bin$.)

    Being able to apply this translation on the fly, expressing any given state
      in whichever alphabet it is most simply expressed, will turn out to be a
      useful trick that simplifies many intermediate calculations. As a bonus,
      it brings to the forefront many of the unique aspects that separate
      quantum algorithms from classical ones, and experience tells us that
      playing around with the dual-alphabet system of notation can be very 
      helpful in building your quantum intuition! 

  \subsection{Dual-Alphabet Pseudocode}
    We have introduced the notation $\register[B] \xstore \register[A]$ for the
      operation that, if register $\register[A]$ is in state $\ket a$ and
      register $\register[B]$ is in state $\ket b$, applies the operation 
      \[
        \ket{a,b}_{\register[AB]} \to \ket{a, b \xor a}_{\register[AB]}\, .
      \]
      If $a$ and $b$ are bits, $a,b \in \{0,1\}$, then this is just a CNOT
      from register $\register[A]$ to register $\register[B]$.
      On the other hand, for any function $f$ that we know how to compute, 
      writing $\register[B] \xstore f(\register[A])$ denotes the standard 
      reversible funciton evaluation 
      \[
        \ket{a,b}_{\register[AB]} \to \ket{a, b \xor f(a)}_{\register[AB]}\, .
      \]

    What are the equivalent operations for Hadamard-basis states? Well, we know
      that the $\Zgate$ (phase-flip) gate plays the role of the $\Xgate$ (not)
      gate in the Hadamard basis; in fact, with the bases being dual, $\Zgate$
      and $\Xgate$ switch roles exactly when moving between the computational
      and Hadamard bases:
      \begin{align*}
        \ket{0} \apply{\Xgate} \ket{1}\, ,\ \ket{1} \apply{\Xgate} \ket{0} 
          &\qquad \ket{0} \apply{\Zgate} \ket{0}\, ,\ \ket{1} \apply{\Zgate} -\ket{1} \\
        \ket{+} \apply{\Zgate} \ket{-}\, ,\ \ket{-} \apply{\Zgate} \ket{+}
          &\qquad \ket{+} \apply{\Xgate} \ket{+}\, ,\ \ket{-} \apply{\Xgate} -\ket{-}
      \end{align*}
      which in our notation may be written more succinctly as
      \begin{align*}
        \ket{b} \apply{\Xgate} \ket{b \xor 1}\, &,\quad \ket{b} \apply{\Zgate} (-1)^b \ket{b} \\
        \ket{\hat{b}} \apply{\Zgate} \ket{\hat{b} \xor \hat{1}}\, &,\quad \ket{\hat{b}} \apply{\Xgate} (-1)^b \ket{\hat{b}}
      \end{align*}
      Staying with single-qubit states for now, we want to identify the
      equivalent of the CNOT operation,
      a.k.a.\ $\CXgate$, in the Hadamard domain, i.e.\ the operation
      \[
        \ket{\hat{a}, \hat{b}} \to \ket{\hat{a}, \hat{b} \xor \hat{a}}\, .
      \]
      This \emph{almost} looks like a controlled-$\Zgate$, or $\CZgate$, except that we
      are controlling on $\ket{\hat a} = \ket{\hat 1}$, in other words that
      register $\register[A]$ is in state $\ket{-}$. Call this operation
      $\hCZgate$; it is defined as:
      \[
        \hCZgate_{\register[AB]} = 
          \ketbra{+}_{\register[A]} \otimes \unitary[I]_{\register[B]}
          + \ketbra{-}_{\register[A]} \otimes \Zgate_{\register[B]}\, ,
      \]
      i.e.\ the unitary that applies $\Zgate$ to register $\register[B]$
        if register $\register[A]$ is in the state $\ket -$, and acts as the identity
        otherwise. Let us see explicitly what happens when we apply $\hCZgate$
        to the state $\ket{\hat a, \hat b}$:
      \begin{multline*}
        \hCZgate \ket{\hat a, \hat b} 
          = \hCZgate \ket{\hat a} \left( \frac{\ket 0 + (-1)^b \ket 1}{\sqrt 2} \right)
          = \ket{\hat a} \left( \frac{\ket 0 + (-1)^b(-1)^a \ket 1}{\sqrt 2} \right) \\
          = \ket{\hat a} \left( \frac{\ket 0 + (-1)^{b \xor a} \ket 1}{\sqrt 2} \right)
          = \ket{\hat a, \widehat{b \xor a}} \eqqcolon \ket{\hat a, \hat{b} \xor \hat{a}}\, ,
      \end{multline*}
      where we have defined the xor between two dual-alphabet strings 
        $\hat a \xor \hat b$ to simply be the dual-alphabet expression of the
        xored value $a \xor b$. We have found the operation we sought: The dual-domain
        equivalent of the CNOT (or $\CXgate$) operation. (This is perhaps even
        easier to see if we write $\Zgate = \hat{\Xgate}$, in which case
        $\hCZgate$ just becomes $\hat{\unitary[C]}\hat\Xgate$.)

      Going back to multi-qubit states, recall that the operation 
        $\outReg \xstore \inReg$ on two equally-sized registers $\inReg$ and
        $\outReg$, denotes the operation:
        \[
          \ket{x,y} \to \ket{x,y \xor x}\, ,
        \]
        where now, $y \xor x$ denotes the bitwise xor of $x$ and $y$
        (or equivalently, the sum $x + y \pmod{2}$, if we choose to
        interpret $x$ and $y$ as integers over $\ZZ_{2^n}$ instead of
        as fixed-length bitstrings over $\bin^n$).
        Hence, this operation can be implemented by simply applying $\CXgate$
        gates between corresponding qubits, so that $\ket{x_i,y_i} \to \ket{x_i,
        y_i \xor x_i}$. It is clear that the corresponding
        Hadamard-domain operation must be to apply $\hCZgate$ between
        the corresponding qubits of $\inReg$ and $\outReg$, so that 
        $\ket{\hat{x}_i, \hat{y}_i} \to \ket{\hat{x}_i, \hat{y}_i \xor
        \hat{x}_i}$, and
        \[
          \ket{\hat x, \hat y} \to \ket{\hat x, \widehat{x \xor y}} 
          \eqqcolon \ket{\hat x, \hat{x} \xor \hat{y}}\, . 
        \]
        Denote this operation $\outReg \zstore \inReg$, to distinguish it from
        the bitwise CNOT operation $\outReg \zstore \inReg$.

  \subsection{Properties of $\xstore$ and $\zstore$.}
    \subsubsection{The Single-Qubit Case.}
      Let us explore how $\xstore$ and $\zstore$ act on various initial states,
        starting with single qubits.
        By design, the $\xstore$ operand takes states $\ket{a, b}$ to
        $\ket{a, b \xor a}$, while 
        $\zstore$ takes states $\ket{\hat a, \hat b}$ to 
        $\ket{\hat a, \hat b \xor \hat a}$. 
        What about the other way around?
        Or for that matter, mixed-alphabet states like $\ket{\hat a, b}$ and
        $\ket{a, \hat b}$? In other words, we would like to fill out the missing

      Let us begin by asking how $\zstore$ affects the computational-basis initial
        state $\ket{a,b}$: $\register[A]$ is a single-qubit register 
        initialized to $\ket a$, and $\register[B]$ is a single-qubit
        register initialized to $\ket b$, and $a,b \in \bin$. 
        
      First, recall that $\zstore$ does not control on $a = 1$, but rather on
        $\register[A]$ being in state $\ket{\hat 1} = \ket{-}$, so start by 
        Hadamard-translating the state $\ket a$ to express it in the
        dual alphabet:
        \[
          \ket{a,b} = \frac{\ket{\hat 0} + (-1)^a \ket{\hat 1}}{\sqrt 2}\ket b\, .
        \]
      For single-qubit registers, the operation $\register[B] \zstore
      \register[A]$ simply applies the $\hCZgate$ operation from $\register[A]$ to $\register[B]$.
      The $\Zgate$ gate applied to a computational-basis state $\ket b$ flips the phase iff 
      $b = 1$, and it is only applied in the $\hat 1$-branch of the
      superposition, so we get:
      \[
        \frac{\ket{\hat 0} + (-1)^a \ket{\hat 1}}{\sqrt 2}\ket b
          \apply{\hCZgate} 
          \frac{\ket{\hat 0} + (-1)^{a} (-1)^{b} \ket{\hat 1}}{\sqrt 2}\ket b
          = \frac{\ket{\hat 0} + (-1)^{a \xor b} \ket{\hat 1}}{\sqrt 2}\ket b\, .
      \]
      Finally, Hadamard-translate the $\register[A]$ state back to the standard alphabet:
      \[
          \frac{\ket{\hat 0} + (-1)^{a \xor b} \ket{\hat 1}}{\sqrt 2}\ket b
          = \ket{a \xor b, b}\, .
      \]
      So, the operand $\zstore$ takes a state $\ket{a,b}$ to the state 
      $\ket{a \xor b, b}$. Since the operands $\zstore$ and $\xstore$ switch
      roles exactly when moving from the computational basis (standard alphabet)
      to the Hadamard basis (dual alphabet), we can also conclude right away
      that the operand $\xstore$ must take the state $\ket{\hat a, \hat b}$ to
      $\ket{\hat a \xor \hat b, \hat b}$.

      Notice the symmetries: First, the table gets a hat/non-hat symmetry
        diagonally from the lower-left to the upper-right, and from the lower-right to the
        upper-left, in the sense that switching $a$, $\hat a$, and $\xstore$ for
        $\hat a$, $\hat b$, and $\zstore$ (or vice versa) leaves the operation
        invariant. This is simply saying that the computational basis and the
        Hadamard basis are duals of each other, with $\xstore$ and $\zstore$
        switching roles between bases.

      The more interesting symmetry is the fact that switching $\xstore$ for
        $\zstore$ and $\register[A]$ for $\register[B]$ \emph{also} leaves the
        operation invariant. In other words, applying $\zstore$ from $\inReg$ to
        $\outReg$ \emph{has the same effect as} applying $\xstore$ from
        $\outReg$ to $\inReg$.
        Since we have shown that this symmetry holds for every state in the qubit
        basis $\{\ket 0, \ket 1\}$, the symmetry must hold for \emph{every}
        qubit state; and since the $\xstore$ and $\zstore$ operations are
        implement bitwise for multi-qubit registers, the same must be true in
        general. %Let us state this as a lemma.

      \begin{lemma}\label{lemma:xzstore}
        Let $\inReg$ and $\outReg$ be $n$-qubit. Then
        applying $\xstore$ from $\inReg$ to $\outReg$ is equivalent to applying
        $\zstore$ from $\outReg$ to $\inReg$, and vice versa:
        \[
        \outReg \xstore \inReg\ \equiv\ \inReg \zstore \outReg\, ,
        \quad
        \inReg \xstore \outReg\ \equiv\ \outReg \zstore \inReg\, .
        \]
      \end{lemma}

      This may seem like something of a surprise, but \cref{lemma:xzstore} is
        actually just a reformulation of the well-known fact that
        CNOT switches direction under the Hadamard transformation -- the target
        becomes the control, and the control becomes the target. Written in
        terms of the unitaries:
      \[
        \Hgate\, \CXgate_{\register[AB]}\, \Hgate = \CXgate_{\register[BA]}\, .
      \]

      Going back to our table, let us investigate multi-alphabet state
        $\ket{a, \hat b}$ next.


        $\ket{x, \hat y}$ and $\ket{\hat x, y}$? How do our two operands affect
        those? Let's find out by once again considering the single-qubit cases.
        To start, an operation that should seem familiar to you: Let
        register $\register[A]$ be in state $\ket{\hat a}$ and register
        $\register[B]$ be in state $\ket b$. Then the operation 
        $\register[B] \xstore \register[A]$ will produce the following state:
        \[
          \ket{\hat a, b} 
            = \frac{\ket{0} + (-1)^a \ket{1}}{\sqrt 2}\ket b\ 
            \apply{\CXgate}\ \frac{\ket{0, b} + (-1)^a \ket{1, b \xor 1}}{\sqrt 2}\, .
        \]
        The result is one of the four maximally-entangled \emph{Bell states}:
        \begin{align*}
          a = 0\, ,\ b = 0\ \implies\ \frac{\ket{00} + \ket{11}}{\sqrt 2} \eqqcolon \ket{\mathrm{\Phi}^+}\, ,
          &\qquad
          a = 1\, ,\ b = 0\ \implies\ \frac{\ket{00} - \ket{11}}{\sqrt 2} \eqqcolon \ket{\mathrm{\Phi}^-} 
          \\
          a = 0\, ,\ b = 1\ \implies\ \frac{\ket{01} + \ket{10}}{\sqrt 2} \eqqcolon \ket{\mathrm{\Psi}^+}\, ,
          &\qquad
          a = 1\, ,\ b = 1\ \implies\ \frac{\ket{01} - \ket{10}}{\sqrt 2} \eqqcolon \ket{\mathrm{\Psi}^-}\, .
        \end{align*}
        The equivalent Hadamard-domain operation is to let register
        $\register[A]$ be in the state $\ket{a}$ and register
        $\register[B]$ start in the state $\ket{\hat b}$, then apply the operation
        $\register[B] \zstore \register[A]$:
        \[
          \ket{a, \hat b} 
            = \frac{\ket{\hat 0} + (-1)^a \ket{\hat 1}}{\sqrt 2}\ket{\hat b}\ 
            \apply{\hCZgate}\ 
            \frac{\ket{\hat 0, \hat b} + (-1)^a \ket{\hat 1, \hat b \xor \hat 1}}{\sqrt 2}\, .
        \]
        Interestingly, using \cref{lemma:xzstore}, we know that this is equivalent to applying the
        operation $\register[A] \xstore \register[B]$, yielding the same Bell
        state as before, except with the roles of $a$ and $b$ swapped:
        \[
          \ket{a, \hat b}\ \to\
            \frac{\ket{a, 0} + (-1)^b \ket{a \xor 1, 1}}{\sqrt 2}\, ,
        \]
        which means that these two states must in fact be identical:
        \[
          \frac{\ket{\hat 0, \hat b} + (-1)^a \ket{\hat 1, \hat b \xor \hat 1}}{\sqrt 2}
          = \frac{\ket{a, 0} + (-1)^b \ket{a \xor 1, 1}}{\sqrt 2}\, .
        \]
        What we just discovered is the well-known fact that the Hadamard
          transformation acts like a permutation on the set of Bell states:
          Inserting for different values of $a$ and $b$, we see that (up to a
          global phase which we ignore):
        \[
          \ket{\mathrm{\Phi}^+} \apply{\Hgate^{\otimes 2}} \ket{\mathrm{\Phi}^+}\, ,
          \quad
          \ket{\mathrm{\Psi}^-} \apply{\Hgate^{\otimes 2}} \ket{\mathrm{\Psi}^-}\, ,
          \quad
          \ket{\mathrm{\Phi}^-} \lrapply{\Hgate^{\otimes 2}} \ket{\mathrm{\Psi}^+}\, .
        \]

      That settles two of the cases of how $\register[B] \xstore \register[A]$
        acts on $\ket{\hat a, b}$  and how $\register[B] \zstore \register[A]$ 
        acts on $\ket{a, \hat b}$. 
        Next up, let us look at how $\register[B] \zstore \register[A]$ 
        affects the starting state $\ket{\hat a, b}$. 
        %As before, we have a choice to make in which basis 
        Since we are applying the $\Zgate$ gate iff $a = 1$ 
        (that is to say, $\hat a = \hat 1$), and since $\Zgate$ acts
        non-trivially on a state $\ket b$ iff $b = 1$ (in which case it picks up
        a minus sign), the result must be that $\register[B] \zstore \register[A]$
        has the effect
        \[
          \ket{\hat a, b} \apply{\hCZgate} (-1)^{a \cdot b}\ket{\hat a, b}\, .
        \]
        The only effect is that the operation gives the state a global phase of $-1$ \emph{if} $a$
        and $b$ are both $1$. With global phases being unphysical, this means that
        in isolation, operation acts on the state like the \emph{identity}.\footnote{
          Another way to see this is that $\ket{\hat a, b}$ is an
          eigenstate of the $\hCZgate$ operator, which follows from
          the fact that $\ket b$ is an eigenstate of the $\Zgate$ operator.
        }
        But of course, we will often \emph{not} apply $\zstore$ in isolation, and
        the moment the operation is controlled on other qubit states (such as
        when appearing inside a $\pcfor \pcbranch$ ``loop''), the phase
        is no longer global, and may certainly \emph{not} be ignored.
        Finally, we may again use \cref{lemma:xzstore} to immediately conclude that the
        operation starting with the state $\ket{\hat{a}, b}$, the operation 
        $\register[A] \xstore \register[B]$ must have the same effect as applying 
        $\register[B] \zstore \register[A]$, which in this case 
        just yields the same state $(-1)^{a \cdot b}\ket{a,b}$
        (since multiplication is commutative).

      That covers the four possible dual-alphabet basis states for two single qubits.

        are perfectly well-defined on their own merits, as one choice among a
        continuous infinity of measurements we could choose to apply.
	      %(we will make this formal in \cref{sec:measurements}).
        Perhaps it is better, then, to view the Hadamard basis as a sort of
        ``dual domain'' under which we can view our qubit,
	      with two possible questions we can ask our state: 
	      Either ``Are you $\ket 0$ or $\ket 1$?'', or 
	      ``Are you $\ket +$ or $\ket -$?'' Either way, 
	      the qubit will provide a definite answer, after which its state will 
        ``collapse'' to the answer you got.

	    This is a point worth internalizing, so let us repeat it: 
	      \emph{The set of possible post-measurement states changes to match
	      the question you chose to ask.} If you ask ``Are you $0$ or $1$?'', 
	      then the post-measurement state will be $\ket 0$ or $\ket 1$; 
	      if you ask ``Are you $+$ or $-$'', then the post-measurement state will
        be $\ket +$ or $\ket -$. 
        So although at one level, the ``collapse'' of the quantum state is just
        the quantum version of how one would use Bayes' theorem 
        to update a prior probability distribution to a marginal distribution
        conditioned on the outcome, at another level, it represents the
        deepest and strangest mystery in all of quantum physics.
        The grand mystery of quantum physics really comes down to this one fact, 
        that the outcome statistics and the post-observation state both depend
        on \emph{which measurement you chose to apply}, and it has led to a century
        of heated philosophical debate over how quantum theory should be
        interpreted. Ask five physicists for their opinion on the matter, and
        you are likely to get five different answers!\footnote{

      What about a question like ``Are you $\ket 0$ or
        are you $\ket +$?'' Under our notion of measurement, this question is not
        well-defined, since $\ket 0$ and $\ket +$ are not orthogonal states and
        therefore do not lead to mutually-exclusive outcomes. More on this in
        \cref{sec:measurements}, but for now, think of a measurement as always
        being defined in terms of a set of mutually-exclusive outcomes.

	    Of course, any vector space has a continuous infinity of 
	      bases to choose from, each of which define a binary ``question'' we
	      can pose to our qubit, and the Hadamard
	      basis is just one particularly nice choice:
	      geometrically, it is just the basis rotated by $45^\circ$
	      relative to the original one, as illustrated in
	      \cref{fig:hadamard-basis}.

	      To appreciate how this makes for a perspective
         ``dual'' to the computational basis,
	      take a look at the following identities:
        \[
          \ket{+} = \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\, , \quad 
          \ket{-} = \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}
          \qquad
          \Longleftrightarrow
          \qquad
          \ket{0} = \frac{\ket{+} + \ket{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\, , \quad 
          \ket{1} = \frac{\ket{+} - \ket{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\, .
        \]
        Notice the symmetry: The computational basis states $\ket 0$ and $\ket 1$
        are to the Hadamard basis \emph{exactly} what the plus state and minus
        states are to the computational basis; they really are \emph{dual views},
        which can be translated between via the map $\Hgate$.\footnote{
          The notion of a ``dual domain'' will sound familiar to anyone who has
          ever come across Fourier analysis, and not without reason: The Hadamard
          map is exactly the single-qubit Fourier transformation.

      How does this duality generalize to multi-qubit states?
        We had that
        \[
          \ket{+^n} 
          = \frac{\ket{0 \ldots 00} + \ket{0 \ldots 01} + \ldots + \ket{1 \ldots 11}}{\sqrt{2^n}}
          = \sum_{x \in \bin^n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\ket x
        \]
        With $\{\ket 0, \ket 1\}$ and $\{\ket +, \ket -\}$ being exact duals of
        each other, then it must also be true that 
        \[
          \ket{0^n} 
          = \frac{\ket{+ \ldots ++} + \ket{+ \ldots +-} + \ldots + \ket{- \ldots --}}{\sqrt{2^n}}
          = \sum_{\hat{x} \in \{+,-\}^n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat x}\, .
        \]
        Here, we have introduced a new kind of bitstring, one that is defined
        over the alphabet $\{+, -\}$ rather than the alphabet $\bin$. We will use the
        convention that whenever a number or variable is represented with a hat,
        then it is understood to be a \emph{Hadamard-bitstring} like above. For example,
        whereas we previously represented the decimal $21$ by the bitstring
        $[21]_2 = 10101$, writing $\widehat{21}$ would imply that it is
        represented by the Hadamard-bitstring $\big[\, \widehat{21}\, \big]_2 = -+-+-$. 

      As we might have anticipated, just like the all-zero state $\ket{0^n}$
        maps to the decimal state $\ket 0$, 
        %and the all-ones state $\ket{1^n}$
        %maps to the decimal $(N-1)$-state $\ket{N-1}$ (where $N = 2^n$ as usual),
        the uniform superposition $\ket{+^n}$
        then maps to the Hadamard-zero state $\ket{\hat 0}$.
        %, while the all-minus state
        %$\ket{-^n}$ maps to the state $\ket{\hat{N}-\hat{1}}$.
        Thus, we recover our dual views on the quantum
        state space, which we can translate between as desired via the Hadamard
        transformation. Defining $\hat{\ZZ}_N \coloneqq \{ \hat{x}\ |\ x \in
        \ZZ_N\}$, and letting the dual-alphabet inner bitstring product
        $\hat x \cdot x$ simply treat $\{+,-\}$ as if it were $\bin$,
        we can then express both directions of the Hadamard transformation in
        purely decimal notation:
        \[
          \ket{\hat x} = \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N} 
          \frac{(-1)^{\hat x \cdot x}}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket x\, ,
          \quad
          \ket{x} = \sum_{\hat x \in \hat{\ZZ}_N} \frac{(-1)^{x \cdot
          \hat x}}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{x}}\, .
        \]
        
      The above notation will at times allow us to greatly simplify our
        notation, making arguments clearer. Consider for example the state
        \[
          \ket{\psi} = 
          -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{0}\
          +
          \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N \backslash \{0\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{x}\, ,
        \]
        i.e.\ the uniform superposition state that singles out the $\ket{0}$ state (implicitly
        understood to be the $\ket{0^n}$ state since we are using decimal notation) 
        and applies a negative phase to it. Computing the Hadamard transformation of
        such a state can lead to a mess of double sums, but using the above
        notation, not only is applying the transformation as simple as adding
        hats everywhere,
        \[
          %\ket{\psi} 
          - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{0}\
          +
          \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N \backslash \{0\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{x}
          \quad
          \apply{\Hgate^{\otimes n}} 
          \quad
          - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{0}}\ +
          \sum_{\hat x \in \hat{\ZZ}_N \backslash \{\hat{0}\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{x}}\, ,
        \]
        but we can then even choose to write out the state using a mix of
        notations, ``unpacking'' the complex-phase term for closer study while keeping 
        the real-valued terms around in their Hadamard-bitstring form:
        \[
          -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{0}}\ +
          \sum_{\hat x \in \hat{\ZZ}_N \backslash \{\hat{0}\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{x}}
          =
          \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N} -\frac{1}{2^n} \ket{x}\ +
          \sum_{\hat x \in \hat{\ZZ}_N \backslash \{\hat{0}\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{x}}\, .
        \]
