\section{\titlePrimer}\label{sec:primer}
  Quantum theory arose as a branch of physics, and so (naturally) much of the
    standard notational conventions are hand-wavy and inconsistently
    implemented across the literature. For the sake of conceptual clarity and
    self-containedness, we will therefore make an effort to specify all our
    working conventions, introducing the
    building blocks that make up quantum computing theory along the way.
    For more details, see the rightly celebrated introductory text by Nielsen and
    Chuang~\cite{NieChu10}, or the equally-excellent book on quantum
    information theory by Mark Wilde~\cite{Wilde17}.

  \subsection{Quantum Physics and the Dirac Notation}\label{sec:dirac}
    In brief: Quantum states are complex-valued vectors in Hilbert space,
      which is to say, a vector space defined over functions. This Hilbert space
      may or may not be infinite-dimensional, though thankfully, we will only have
      to contend with the finite-dimensional case.\footnote{
        For an example of an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, consider the
        state $\ket{p(x)}$ describing the physical position $x \in [0,1]$ of some
        particle $p$. Since space is not (or not known to be) discretized,
        observing this state may have infinitely
        many possible outcomes: one for every real number between $0$ and $1$. Hence, $\ket{p(x)}$ lives
        in the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space spanned by the infinite set of basis
        states $\{\ket{p(x)}\}_{x\in[0,1]}$.
      }
    %
    Quantum states are represented using the Dirac notation, in which a quantum state
      carrying the \emph{label} $\psi$ is written $\ket{\psi}$, its conjugate
      transpose is written $\bra{\psi} \coloneqq \ket{\psi}^\dagger \coloneqq
      (\ket{\psi}^*)^T$, and its
      inner product with itself is written $\braket{\psi|\psi} \coloneqq
      \ket{\psi}^\dagger \ket{\psi} = \abs{\ket{\psi}}^2$. Quantum states are unit-norm, and so we have that
      $\braket{\psi|\psi} = 1$. For two not-necessarily-equal quantum states
      $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\varphi}$, their inner product
      $\braket{\psi|\varphi}$ is some complex number $\alpha$ whose norm
      $\abs{\alpha}$ sits between zero and one, where
      ``zero'' means that the states are orthogonal, and ``one'' means that the
      states are identical (modulo an unobservable global phase factor $e^{i\theta}$).

    This calculus is fine, but one may wonder, what \emph{is} a quantum
      state? Meaning, what kind of mathematical object is it? The answer
      can be found in the statement that they live in a possibly-infinite-dimensional 
      \emph{Hilbert space,} which are vector spaces defined over
      \emph{functions.}. Here, the inner product between two functions $f$ and
      $g$ on the interval $[a,b]$ is defined as $\int_{a}^{b}
      f(x)^* g(x)\, dx$; this inner product defines a norm, as well as a notion of
      orthogonality, from which a vector space may be constructed.
      Quantum states, then, are the functions of this Hilbert space, arising as
      solutions to some differential equation describing the dynamics of the system. 
      One such differential equation is the famous Schrödinger equation, 
      which covers the non-relativistic, non-spin case. 
      For fermions like electrons (which do have spin), we need the
      \emph{Pauli equation}, and making quantum physics consistent with
      Einstein's theory of special relitivity further requires the \emph{Dirac
      equation}.\footnote{
        Of course, combining quantum physics with \emph{general}
        relativity constitutes \emph{the} open problem of theoretical physics,
        and has given us a half-century of stagnant debate over which
        as-yet unsuccessful approach (string theory, loop quantum gravity,
        \ldots) is bound to one day grant us the coveted Theory of Everything. 
        (For more on this, see the popular science
        section of your local book store.)
      } These differential equations happen to describe the dynamics of waves,
      and so their solutions are often referred to as the ``wave function'' of
      the system. They take some input value corresponding to a possible
      observation, such as a particular position $x$, and returns an
      \emph{amplitude} corresponding to that position; squaring this amplitude
      will then give you the probability that the particle
      is observed to be in position $x$.

    If all of this sounds terribly complicated, then you may appreciate why
      physicists grew so fond of Dirac's notation, which neatly sweeps the
      heavy machinery under a rug of neat, algebraic clarity.

    Thankfully, things are much simpler in the discrete case: the Hilbert
      space is now finite, and so it can be expressed in terms of a finite set of basis
      ``vectors'' (i.e.\ finite-domain functions), and inner
      products are now defined in terms of sums rather than integrals. 
      %Further, since our ``wave functions'' now have finite domains, they may be
      %written as $N$-length vectors
      Each element of a ``state vector'' corresponds to one of the possible inputs to
      the function (i.e., one of the possible observable outcomes), enabling us to
      employ our toolbox from linear algebra to study them, leveraging
      the isomorphism between the set of functions from $\bin^n$ to $\CC$ 
      and $\CC^{2^n}$. 
      This is the approach adopted in the quantum information sciences.

    You may have noticed that if the input to our system is an $n$-length
      bitstring, then the corresponding quantum state vector will be of length
      $2^n$, since vector elements correspond to the possible inputs to the
      function. From one point of view, this is no more mysterious than
      the fact that a discrete probability distribution can be expressed as an
      exponential-length vector, listing one probability for each element in its
      domain; in fact, you may find it helpful to think of quantum state vectors
      as a kind of generalized probability distribution. 

    Another view is that it is exactly in this exponentiality of state vectors
      that the power of quantum
      computation lies. This is because, unlike classical probabilities, amplitudes may
      \emph{interfere} with each other, cancelling and reinforcing exactly like water
      waves meeting in a pond, opening a whole new world of calculation
      possibilities. And yet, at the end of the day, what we actually observe 
      will still be a probability distribution, derived from the squares of
      the amplitudes. It is like nature gave us \emph{some} limited access to
      the exponential nature of quantum physics, yet stopping short of letting us
      observe it directly, lest our power grow too great.\footnote{
        If we could directly access the ever-splitting branches of 
        quantum states, and pick and choose the outcomes we would most like to
        see rather than hope that they appear by chance upon measurement,
        then we could easily solve NP-complete problems~\cite{Aaronson05}.
      }

  \subsection{Basis States}
    \subsubsection{Single-Qubit Basis States.}
      We are interested in modelling quantum computers. A quantum computer must, of
        course, have some quantum memory, which will in our case be a collection of
        qubits; let's say $n$ of them. 
        This memory can then at any point in time be said to be in some quantum state
        defined over $n$ qubits. 

      A \emph{qubit} is a physical system which, when observed, can be observed
        to take one of two possible values. 
        In physical implementations, these observables may for
        example correspond to the spin of an electron (``up'' and ``down''),
        or to the two lowest energy states of an atom; in general they may be
        any two orthogonal states of an observable, where two quantum states are
        orthogonal if and only if there exists a measurement\footnote{
          The words ``observation'' and ``measurement'' describe the same
          procedure. We prefer ``observation'' when speaking informally,
          as the term captures the fact that you don't need fancy lab equipment to
          collapse a quantum state -- coming in contact with it in any way 
          is enough -- but we will follow the standard nomenclature of ``measurement''
          when talking about the technical formalism.
        } procedure that perfectly distinguishes them.

      With exactly two possible outcomes, the qubit \emph{state} lives
        in the two-dimensional Hilbert space $\CC^2$, which we characterize by
        the basis states $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$. 
        %similarly to how two orthogonal unit vectors will span the real plane. 
        It is worth emphasizing that in these states, the numbers ``$0$'' and ``$1$'' 
        are \emph{labels}, and do not say anything about the physical
        system in question other than that it supports (at least) two orthogonal
        quantum states, which we have the
        ability to distinguish, and which we \emph{choose} to interpret as the
        symbols $0$ and $1$.
        In particular, the $\ket 0$ state could certainly not be the zero
        \emph{vector}, since all quantum states must be unit-length for
        probabilities to sum to one, and the zero vector has zero length. 

      Let $\ket{b_1}$ and $\ket{b_2}$ be two qubit basis states, where $b_1, b_2 \in
        \{0,1\}$. Since these must be either identical or orthogonal, we have that
      \[
        \braket{b_1|b_2} = \delta_{b_1 b_2}\, ,
      \]
      where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta (equal to $1$ if $i = j$ and $0$
        otherwise).
        Expressing the qubit states (which, remember, are functions from a binary
        set of observable values to a complex number with magnitude at most
        $1$) as vectors, we see that we can express our qubit basis states as
        the column vectors
        \[
          \ket0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\, ,\ 
          \ket1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\, .
        \]
        Then, their conjugate transposes become row vectors:
        \begin{align*}
          \bra0 &= \ket0^{\dagger} = (\ket0^*)^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\, ,\ \\
          \bra1 &= \ket1^{\dagger} = (\ket1^*)^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\, .
        \end{align*}
        As a sanity check, the inner product of two single-qubit basis states is
        then
        \[
          \braket{b_1|b_2} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 && 1-b_1 \end{bmatrix}
            \begin{bmatrix} b_2 \\ 1-b_2 \end{bmatrix}
              = b_1 b_2 + (1-b_1)(1-b_2) 
              %= 2 b_1 b_2 - b_1 - b_2 + 1 
              = \delta_{b_1 b_2}\, ,
        \]
        as desired.

      If you are ever confused about a derivation using these Dirac
        ``bra-kets'', it can be very useful to make the translation into vectors
        and matrices, and go through the
        explicit calculation using the familiar language of linear algebra.
        \cref{tab:notation} provides a helpful overview of our conventions and
        their definitions.
      
    \subsubsection{Multi-Qubit Basis States.}
      In general, two quantum systems (i.e.\ states describing 
        distinct physical systems, such as two qubits) may be combined via the
        tensor product, for which we may use either of the following notational
        conventions:
        \[
          \ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{\varphi} \coloneqq \ket{\psi}\ket{\varphi}
          \coloneqq \ket{\psi, \varphi} \coloneqq \ket{\psi\varphi}\, .
        \]
  
      \noindent
      If $\ket{\psi}$ describes an $N$-dimensional quantum system, and
        $\ket{\varphi}$ describes an $M$-dimensional quantum system, then the
        combined state $\ket{\psi, \varphi}$ describing the combined system will be
        $NM$-dimensional. (Note how dimensions are \emph{multiplied} rather
        than \emph{added}, as one might have na\"ively expected; the addition
        happens in the \emph{qubit count}, which lives in the exponent.)
  
      Given a collection of $n$ qubits $\ket{b_i}$, each initialized to
        one of the two basis states $\ket0$ or $\ket1$ (so that $b_i \in \bin$), we may construct
        the combined state 
        \begin{align*}
          \ket{b_0} \otimes \ket{b_1} \otimes \ldots \otimes \ket{b_{n-1}}
          &\coloneqq \ket{b_0} \ket{b_1} \ldots \ket{b_{n-1}} \\
          &\coloneqq \ket{b_0, b_1, \ldots, b_{n-1}} \\
          &\coloneqq \ket{b_0 b_1 \ldots b_{n-1}}\, .
        \end{align*}

      \noindent
      We will use any and all of the above conventions when denoting combined quantum
        states, choosing whichever one we deem clearest 
        for the given context. For example, non-binary labels like $\psi$ and
        $\varphi$ will typically be separated by commas, while binary-valued labels will
        typically not be, to match the standard nomenclature for bit-string
        concatenation: $b_0 b_1 \coloneqq b_0 \concat b_1$.

      Since each qubit is a two-dimensional quantum state, and there are $n$
        qubits, the
        combined state will have dimension $N \coloneqq 2^n$, and the resulting Hilbert
        space will be characterized by the basis states
      \[
        \{\ket{i}\}_{i \in [N]} = \{ \ket{b_0 b_1 \ldots b_{n-1}}
          \}_{b_0, \ldots, b_{n-1} \in \bin}\, .
      \]
      Of course, any vector space permits an infinitude of basis
        choices by rotating the basis vectiors; the above choice of basis is
        just the simplest one. We refer
        to this as the ``computational basis'', since
        we have set our basis vectors up in such a way that they map to
        classical bitstrings. (We will meet another common choice of basis
        in \cref{sec:uniform} below.)

      As indicated, the fact that the dimension of quantum states increases exponentially as
        the number of qubits increases sits at the core of quantum computers'
        advantage over classical computers. An illustrative
        example is the simple three-qubit system: for two qubits, we only needed
        four basis states, but for three qubits this has now doubled to eight:
      \begin{align*}
        \{ \ket{i} \}_{i \in [2^3]} 
        &= \{\ket{000},
          \ket{001},
          \ket{010},
          \ket{011},
          \ket{100},
          \ket{101},
          \ket{110},
          \ket{111}\} \\
        &= \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
          \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
          \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
          \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
          \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
          \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
          \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
          \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\, .
      \end{align*}

      \noindent
      As you can see, multi-qubit basis states also satisfy orthonormality:
      \[ 
        \braket{x|y} = \delta_{xy}\, . 
      \]

      Finally, it will sometimes be useful to perform a repeated tensor product over identical
        states, for instance when initializing $n$ qubits to the $\ket{0}$
        state; this can be written in either of the following ways:
      \[
        \bigotimes_{i = 0}^{n-1} \ket{0} \coloneqq
        \ket{0}^{\otimes n} \coloneqq \ket{0^n} \coloneqq \underbrace{\ket{0 \ldots 0}}_{n}\, .
      \]

  \subsection{Uniform Superposition States}\label{sec:uniform}
      Of course, this is \emph{quantum} computing, and so we are interested in
        qubit states which are more than just classical bitstrings written in
        kets. The unique thing about qubits is that they can be in
        \emph{superpositions} of $\ket 0$ and $\ket 1$, usually popularized to
        the qubit somehow being ``both $0$ and $1$ at once'', before inevitably
        failing to explain what makes this situation any different from simply
        being ignorant about the qubit's ``true'' value until we observe it,
        which is just stochastic computing. Let us demonstrate.

    \subsubsection{Plus and Minus.}
      For a single qubit, the simplest superposition states are common enough to
        warrant their own names: they are the
        ``plus'' and ``minus'' states
        \[
          \ket{+} \coloneqq \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\, , \quad 
          \ket{-} \coloneqq \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\, .
        \]
        If you were to observe these states directly, you would see $0$ or $1$
        with equal probability (since $\prob{\text{observed } 0} =
        \prob{\text{observed } 1} = (\nicefrac{1}{\sqrt 2})^2 = \half$),
        and if this were all, then these states would be no different from the
        probability distribution of a coin-toss.

      What makes quantum superpositions different from classical stochastics is
        that despite both states being uniform superpositions of $0$ and $1$,
        they can be \emph{perfectly distinguished from each other}. This is
        because, unlike the classical world, we are no longer stuck with only
        computational-basis measurements. 

      One of the elementary quantum operations that we can apply to a qubit is
        the \emph{Hadamard gate} $\Hgate$, and it applies the self-inverse map
      \[
        \ket{0} \apply{\Hgate} \ket{+} \apply{\Hgate} \ket{0}\, , \quad
        \ket{1} \apply{\Hgate} \ket{-} \apply{\Hgate} \ket{1}\, .
      \]
      So, if we are given a qubit, and we are told that it is either in the
        state $\ket +$ or in the state $\ket -$, then
        measuring the qubit directly (in the computational basis) 
        would give us no information about which
        of the two it was in. But if we \emph{first} apply $\Hgate$ and \emph{then}
        measure it, then we learn with certainty which state it was in,
        since now observing $0$ can only happen if we were given the state $\ket +$,
        and observing $1$ can only happen if we were given the state $\ket -$.

      We will refer to the procedure of first applying a Hadamard gate to a
        qubit and then measuring it in the computational basis as a
        ``Hadamard-basis measurement'', for reasons that will become clear
        shortly.

    \subsubsection{The Hadamard Transformation.}
      For $n$ qubits, the uniform superposition over every length-$n$ bitstring is the
      state
      \[
        \frac{\ket{0 \ldots 00}
        + \ket{0 \ldots 01} + \ldots + \ket{1 \ldots 11}}{\sqrt{2^n}}
        = 
        \sum_{x \in \bin^n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\ket{x}\, .
      \]
      As it happens, this is equal to the state
      \[
        \ket{+}^{\otimes n}
        \coloneqq 
        \ket{+}\ket{+} \ldots \ket{+} 
        = 
        \left( \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
        \left( \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
        \ldots
        \left( \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}} \right)\, ,
      \]
      since multiplying out the paranthesis gives a sum over all possible
      bitstrings. This is exactly the state that we will get if
      we have the apply the gate $\Hgate$ to each of $n$ qubits in the $\ket 0$
      state:
      \[
        \ket{0^n} \apply{\Hgate} \ket{+^n} \apply{\Hgate} \ket{0^n}\, .
      \]
      We will refer to the procedure of applying the $\Hgate$ operation to every
        qubit of a system as the \emph{Hadamard transformation}. 
      
      What happens do we get if we start with computational basis states other
        than $0^n$? Say we have five qubits, arranged to represent
        the decimal $21$:
        \[
          \ket{[21]_2} = \ket{10101}\, .
        \]
        After applying $\Hgate$ gates to each qubit, we get the state
        \[
          \ket{-+-+-} =
          \left( \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          \left( \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          \left( \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          \left( \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          \left( \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          = \sum_{x \in \bin^5} \frac{(-1)^{x \cdot [21]_2}}{\sqrt{2^5}} \ket x\, ,
        \]
        where $x \cdot [21]_2$ denotes the inner product of the two bitstrings
        modulo $2$ (so for example, $[5]_2 \cdot [21]_2$ evaluates to 
        $00101 \cdot 10101 = 0 \cdot 1 \xor 0 \cdot 0 \xor 1 \cdot 1 \xor 0
        \cdot 0 \xor 1 \cdot 1 = 0$, so the branch $\ket 5$ will have a positive phase).
        This is also a uniform superposition, and measuring it directly
        would again just give the uniform distribution. Yet nothing is lost: 
        all the information about the original number $21$ is preserved in
        the \emph{relative phases} of the different branches of the
        superposition, and can be recovered exactly by
        applying the Hadamard transformation a second time.
        Generalizing, we can write the effect of the Hadamard transformation on
        a computational-basis state as
        \[
          \ket x 
          \apply{\Hgate^{\otimes n}}
          \sum_{y \in \bin^n} \frac{(-1)^{x \cdot y}}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket y\, .
        \]
      The multi-qubit Hadamard-basis measurement is then the procedure that
        first applies the Hadamard transformation to the state, then measures it
        in the computational basis.

    \subsubsection{The Hadamard Basis.}
      Actually, we don't have to imagine explicitly applying the $\Hgate$ gates
        to our qubits before measuring them, as Hadamard-basis measurements
        are perfectly well-defined on their own merits, as one choice among a
        continuous infinity of measurements we could choose to apply.
	      %(we will make this formal in \cref{sec:measurements}).
        Perhaps it is better, then, to view the Hadamard basis as a sort of
        ``dual domain'' under which we can view our qubit,
	      with two possible questions we can ask our state: 
	      Either ``Are you $\ket 0$ or $\ket 1$?'', or 
	      ``Are you $\ket +$ or $\ket -$?'' Either way, 
	      the qubit will provide a definite answer, after which its state will 
        ``collapse'' to the answer you got.

	    This is a point worth internalizing, so let us repeat it: 
	      \emph{The set of possible post-measurement states changes to match
	      the question you chose to ask.} If you ask ``Are you $0$ or $1$?'', 
	      then the post-measurement state will be $\ket 0$ or $\ket 1$; 
	      if you ask ``Are you $+$ or $-$'', then the post-measurement state will
        be $\ket +$ or $\ket -$. 
        So although at one level, the ``collapse'' of the quantum state is just
        the quantum version of how one would use Bayes' theorem 
        to update a prior probability distribution to a marginal distribution
        conditioned on the outcome, at another level, it represents the
        deepest and strangest mystery in all of quantum physics.
        The grand mystery of quantum physics really comes down to this one fact, 
        that the outcome statistics and the post-observation state both depend
        on \emph{which measurement you chose to apply}, and it has led to a century
        of heated philosophical debate over how quantum theory should be
        interpreted. Ask five physicists for their opinion on the matter, and
        you are likely to get five different answers!\footnote{

        being defined in terms of a set of mutually-exclusive outcomes.

	      bases to choose from, each of which define a binary ``question'' we
	      can pose to our qubit, and the Hadamard
	      basis is just one particularly nice choice:
	      geometrically, it is just the basis rotated by $45^\circ$

        \[
          \ket{+} = \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\, , \quad 
          \ket{-} = \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}
          \qquad
          \Longleftrightarrow
          \qquad
          \ket{0} = \frac{\ket{+} + \ket{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\, , \quad 
          \ket{1} = \frac{\ket{+} - \ket{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\, .
        \]
        Notice the symmetry: The computational basis states $\ket 0$ and $\ket 1$
        are to the Hadamard basis \emph{exactly} what the plus state and minus
        states are to the computational basis; they really are \emph{dual views},
        which can be translated between via the map $\Hgate$.\footnote{
          The notion of a ``dual domain'' will sound familiar to anyone who has

      How does this duality generalize to multi-qubit states?
        We had that
        \[
          \ket{+^n} 
          = \frac{\ket{0 \ldots 00} + \ket{0 \ldots 01} + \ldots + \ket{1 \ldots 11}}{\sqrt{2^n}}
          = \sum_{x \in \bin^n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\ket x
        \]
        With $\{\ket 0, \ket 1\}$ and $\{\ket +, \ket -\}$ being exact duals of
        each other, then it must also be true that 
        \[
          \ket{0^n} 
          = \frac{\ket{+ \ldots ++} + \ket{+ \ldots +-} + \ldots + \ket{- \ldots --}}{\sqrt{2^n}}
          = \sum_{\hat{x} \in \{+,-\}^n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat x}\, .
        \]
        Here, we have introduced a new kind of bitstring, one that is defined
        over the alphabet $\{+, -\}$ rather than the alphabet $\bin$. We will use the
        convention that whenever a number or variable is represented with a hat,
        then it is understood to be a \emph{Hadamard-bitstring} like above. For example,
        whereas we previously represented the decimal $21$ by the bitstring
        $[21]_2 = 10101$, writing $\widehat{21}$ would imply that it is
        represented by the Hadamard-bitstring $\big[\, \widehat{21}\, \big]_2 = -+-+-$. 

      As we might have anticipated, just like the all-zero state $\ket{0^n}$
        maps to the decimal state $\ket 0$, 
        %and the all-ones state $\ket{1^n}$
        %maps to the decimal $(N-1)$-state $\ket{N-1}$ (where $N = 2^n$ as usual),
        the uniform superposition $\ket{+^n}$
        then maps to the Hadamard-zero state $\ket{\hat 0}$.
        %, while the all-minus state
        %$\ket{-^n}$ maps to the state $\ket{\hat{N}-\hat{1}}$.
        Thus, we recover our dual views on the quantum
        state space, which we can translate between as desired via the Hadamard
        transformation. Defining $\hat{\ZZ}_N \coloneqq \{ \hat{x}\ |\ x \in
        \ZZ_N\}$, and letting the dual-alphabet inner bitstring product
        $\hat x \cdot x$ simply treat $\{+,-\}$ as if it were $\bin$,
        we can then express both directions of the Hadamard transformation in
        purely decimal notation:
        \[
          \ket{\hat x} = \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N} 
          \frac{(-1)^{\hat x \cdot x}}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket x\, ,
          \quad
          \ket{x} = \sum_{\hat x \in \hat{\ZZ}_N} \frac{(-1)^{x \cdot
          \hat x}}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{x}}\, .
        \]
        
      The above notation will at times allow us to greatly simplify our
        notation, making arguments clearer. Consider for example the state
        \[
          \ket{\psi} = 
          -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{0}\
          +
          \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N \backslash \{0\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{x}\, ,
        \]
        i.e.\ the uniform superposition state that singles out the $\ket{0}$ state (implicitly
        understood to be the $\ket{0^n}$ state since we are using decimal notation) 
        and applies a negative phase to it. Computing the Hadamard transformation of
        such a state can lead to a mess of double sums, but using the above
        notation, not only is applying the transformation as simple as adding
        hats everywhere,
        \[
          %\ket{\psi} 
          - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{0}\
          +
          \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N \backslash \{0\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{x}
          \quad
          \apply{\Hgate^{\otimes n}} 
          \quad
          - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{0}}\ +
          \sum_{\hat x \in \hat{\ZZ}_N \backslash \{\hat{0}\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{x}}\, ,
        \]
        but we can then even choose to write out the state using a mix of
        notations, ``unpacking'' the complex-phase term for closer study while keeping 
        the real-valued terms around in their Hadamard-bitstring form:
        \[
          -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{0}}\ +
          \sum_{\hat x \in \hat{\ZZ}_N \backslash \{\hat{0}\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{x}}
          =
          \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N} -\frac{1}{2^n} \ket{x}\ +
          \sum_{\hat x \in \hat{\ZZ}_N \backslash \{\hat{0}\}} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \ket{\hat{x}}\, .
        \]

  \subsection{General Superposition States}
    \subsubsection{Greek vs.\ Latin Letters.}
      To distinguish computational basis states from more general quantum states, we will use
        Latin letters like $x$, $y$, $z$ to represent classical binary
        strings. Thus, a state $\ket{x}$ will always be understood to be a
        computational basis state. Whenever we are talking specifically about single-qubit
        computational basis states, we will use $b$ to represent a single bit, 
        and write $\ket{b}$. 

      General quantum states, on the other hand, are traditionally labelled by
        letters taken from the middle of the Greek alphabet, such as $\psi$,
        $\varphi$, $\xi$, etc.  We will follow this tradition whenever 
        there isn't an obvious descriptive label to be used for the state.

      Quantum \emph{amplitudes}, meanwhile, are traditionally represented with letters 
        from the start of the Greek alphabet, such as $\alpha$ or $\beta$, and
        the amplitude corresponding to a particular basis state $\ket x$ is typically written
        $\alpha_x$.

    \subsubsection{Expanding a State in Terms of a Basis.} 
      A general single-qubit state may be written in terms of the computational
        basis states as follows:
      \[
        \ket{\psi} = \alpha_0 \ket{0} + \alpha_1 \ket{1}\, ,
      \]
      where the amplitudes $\alpha_b$ are complex numbers satisfying
      \[
        \abs{\alpha_0}^2 + \abs{\alpha_1}^2 
          \coloneqq \alpha_0^*\alpha_0 + \alpha_1^*\alpha_1 = 1\, . 
      \]

      As already covered, whenever $\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_1$ are both non-zero, 
        the qubit is in a superposition over the $0$ and
        $1$ values. If we observe the state via a computational-basis
        measurement, the probability of outcome $0$ resp.\ $1$ is given by
        the respective amplitude squared: 
      \[
        \prob{\, \text{outcome } b\, } = \abs{\alpha_b}^2 
          = \alpha_b^* \alpha_b = \abs{\braket{\psi |b}}^2\, .
      \]

      Likewise, a general, $n$-qubit quantum state may be written in terms of the computational
        basis states:
      \[
        \ket{\psi} 
          = \alpha_0 \ket{0 \dots 00} + \alpha_1 \ket{0 \dots 01} + \ldots +
          \alpha_{N-1} \ket{1 \dots 11}
          = \alpha_0 \ket{0} + \alpha_1 \ket{1} + \ldots +
          \alpha_{N-1} \ket{N-1}
          = \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N} \alpha_x \ket{x}\, ,
      \]
      where we have again made the switch to decimal notation.
      %l, which we could do
      %since the number of qubits was clear from context (so that the bitstring $0^n$ could be
      %unambiguously identified with the number $0$, and so on).
      %, and where the amplitudes $\alpha_i$ again satisfy $\sum_{i = 0}^{N-1} \abs{\alpha_i}^2 = 1$.

      When expanded over the basis states, the inner product of two multi-qubit quantum
        states $\ket{\psi} = \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N} \alpha_x \ket{x}$ and $\ket{\varphi} =
        \sum_{y \in \ZZ_N} \beta_y \ket{y}$ may be written
      \[
        \braket{\psi|\varphi} = \sum_{x, y \in \ZZ_N} \alpha^*_x \beta_y \braket{x|y} =
        \sum_{x, y \in \ZZ_N} \alpha^*_x \beta_y \delta_{xy} = \sum_{x \in \ZZ_N} \alpha^*_x \beta_x\, .
      \]
      We have recovered the inner product of two complex-valued vectors, which
        is just the usual vector inner product with an extra complex conjugation.
        %\footnote{
        %  In case you're wondering, this is because an inner product on any
        %  vector space must satisfy
        %  conjugate symmetry, $\langle x, y \rangle = \langle y, x \rangle^*$;
        %  see \url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product_space}.
        %}
        We may therefore adopt the usual operational interpretation of the inner
        product, in which two unit vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is $0$
        and parallel if it is $1$. Together with the requirement that
        quantum states have unit norm, this means that if two quantum states
        have an inner product of $1$, then they are actually \emph{identical} \ldots
        up to an unobservable phase factor.

    \subsubsection{The Set of Quantum States.}
      Until now, we have used that quantum states $\ket\psi$ live in the Hilbert
        space $\Hspace$, which is correct but not very precise: The
        Hilbert space of $N$-dimensional complex vectors include vectors of arbitrary
        norm, whereas our quantum (pure) states are always unit-length. 
        
      In most cases, this is not really a problem, but what if we want to quantify over
        all possible quantum states for our system? Writing $\forall\, \ket\psi \in \Hspace$ 
        won't cut it, since this would seem to imply that $\ket\psi$ can take
        any norm.
        %break the convention that $\ket\psi$ denotes pure quantum states. 
        We might instead want to write something like
        \[
          \forall\, \ket\psi \in \mathcal{S}(\Hspace) \coloneqq 
          \{ \ket{\psi} \in \Hspace\ |\, \norm{\ket{\psi}} = 1 \}\, ,
          %= \mathcal{S}(\Hspace)\, ,
        \]
        i.e.\ quantify over the unit sphere of $\Hspace$.
        However, you saw in \cref{ex:global-phase} that quantum states that only
        differ by a global phase factor cannot be distinguished by observation, so
        quantifying over the unit sphere would lead to double-counting -- again,
        not a problem most of the time, but not very satisfactory either. 
        Can we do better? 

      What we really want is a set that contains exactly \emph{one} element 
        per observationally-distinct quantum state.
        The standard way to construct such a set is to invoke the 
        machinery of equivalence classes. Let $\sim$ be the equivalence relation
        given by 
      \[
        \ket\psi \sim \ket\varphi\ \Longleftrightarrow\ 
        \exists\, {\theta\, \in[0,2\pi)} \text{ s.th. } \ket\psi = e^{i\theta} \ket\varphi\, ,
      \]
      i.e.\ $\ket\psi \sim \ket\varphi$ iff the two states only differ by a
      global phase factor. Then let $[\ket\psi]$ denote the corresponding
      equivalence class:
      \[
        [\ket\psi] \coloneqq \{ \ket\varphi\, |\, \ket\varphi \sim \ket\psi \}\, ,
        %= \{ \ket\varphi\ |\ \exists_{\theta\, \in[0,2\pi)} e^{i\theta} \ket\psi = \ket\varphi \}\, ,
      \]
      i.e.\ the set of states observationally equivalent to $\ket\psi$.
      Then the set of observationally-distinct quantum states is captured by the set of
      equivalence classes of states on the unit sphere of the Hilbert space $\Hspace$:
      \[
        \states \coloneqq \mathcal{S}(\Hspace)/{\sim} = \{[\ket\psi]\ |\,
        \ket\psi \in \mathcal{S}(\Hspace) \}\, .
      \]
      We may then write $\forall \ket{\psi} \in \states$, and be sure that we
        only quantify over each observably-distinct state only once,
        %by doing the standard trick of 
        leaving implicit the fact that we are
        actually quantifying over \emph{equivalence classes} of objects rather
        than the objects themselves, which is well-defined
        exactly because ``differ by at most a global phase'' and
        ``cannot be distinguished by any measurement'' perfectly imply each other.

    If the qubit a quantum state is describing is not clear from context,
      we may use subscripts to specify which qubit it describes: 
      For example, if $\ket{\psi}$ describes the state of the $i$'th qubit,
      we may write $\ket{\psi}_i$.

      and say we want to put the $i$'th qubit in some single-qubit state $\ket{\psi}$ and the
      $j$'th qubit in another state $\ket{\varphi}$. We could write this
      operation as
    \begin{align*}
      \ket{0}^{\otimes n} 
      %= \ket{0}^{\otimes i} \ket{0} 
      %\ket{0}^{\otimes (j-i)} \ket{0} 
      %\ket{0}^{\otimes (n-j)}
      &\longrightarrow 
      \ket{0}^{\otimes i} \ket{\psi} 
      \ket{0}^{\otimes (j-i)} \ket{\varphi} 
      \ket{0}^{\otimes (n-j)}\, ,

    \[
      \ket{0}_i \ket{0}_j \longrightarrow \ket{\psi}_i \ket{\varphi}_j\, ,
    \]
    where by convention the operation acts like the identity on the state of any

  \subsection{Unitaries}\label{sec:unitaries}
      \hhnote{readthrough cont.}
    Since we are working over finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, 
      operations that evolve our quantum state vectors may be represented as 
      $N \times N$ matrices. What properties should they have?
    
    Clearly, any operation that we perform on a quantum state 
      should yield another valid quantum state. 
      Since quantum states must be unit-norm, this means
      that any operation we perform on them
      (other than measuring -- we will get back to that) 
      must be norm-preserving.
      This fact turns out to be sufficient
      to restrict the space of allowed operations to the unitary matrices,
      i.e.\ the set of invertible square matrices $\unitary$ that satisfy
      $\unitary^{-1} = \unitary^\dagger$.

    \begin{exercise}\label{ex:unitarity}
      Show that a square matrix $\unitary$ acting on a state vector $\ket \psi$ is norm-preserving if
      and only if it is unitary.\\
      \emph{Hint: You will need the identities 
      $\abs{\ket{\psi}} = \sqrt{\braket{\psi|\psi}}$ 
      and $(\unitary \ket{\psi})^\dagger = \bra{\psi}\unitary^\dagger$.}
      %(See \cref{sec:solutions:unitarity} for the solution.)
    \end{exercise}

      %We will use (sans-serif) capital Latin letters to refer to quantum operations, in
      %  reference to their matrix representation.
      %(see \cref{tab:notation} for a summary of our notational conventions). 
    Whenever which qubit or qubit register
      operation is being applied to is not clear from context, we specify using
      subscripts. For example, $\unitary_i$ would denote a
      single-qubit operation applied to qubit $i$, $\unitary_{i,j}$ would
      denote a two-qubit operation applied to qubit $i$ and $j$
      (where generally $\unitary_{i,j} \neq \unitary_{j,i}$), 
      and $\unitary_{\register}$ would denote an operation applied to
      register $\register$.

    It is also often useful to refer to operations in terms of the effect they have
      on the state, and so we define $\ket{\psi} \apply{\unitary} \ket{\psi^\prime}$ to
      mean that we transform the state according to 
      $\ket{\psi} \to \ket{\psi^\prime} = \unitary \ket{\psi}$.

    Here are some common, elementary qubit unitaries, known as single-qubit
    \emph{gates}, in reference to the logic gates of logical circuits:
    \[
      \Xgate = \begin{sqmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{sqmatrix}\, , \quad
      \Ygate = \begin{sqmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{sqmatrix}\, , \quad
      \Zgate = \begin{sqmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{sqmatrix}\, , \quad
      \Hgate = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{sqmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{sqmatrix}\, ,
    \]
    %where the matrices shown are the computational-basis representations of the general
    %  unitary transformations.
    where $\Xgate$ is known as the ``not-gate'', and the $\Xgate, \Ygate,
    \Zgate$ gates are collectively known as the Pauli matrices;
    the $\Hgate$ is known as the Hadamard gate, and is the fundamental
    superposition-generating gate.

    \subsubsection{Transformations across Space and Time.}
      You may have noticed that we have been dealing with two different kinds of
        product for unitary matrices: the usual matrix product
        $\unitary \unitary[V]$, and the tensor product 
        $\unitary \otimes \unitary[V]$. What does each kind of product represent?

      To illustrate, let $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\varphi}$ be two single-qubit
        states, combined in the two-qubit state $\ket{\psi, \varphi}$.
        Then, if $\unitary$ and $\unitary[V]$ are single-qubit operations,
        so represented by $2 \times 2$ unitary matrices, 
        and you want to apply $\unitary$ to $\ket{\psi}$ and
        $\unitary[V]$ to $\ket{\varphi}$, this would be equivalent to the
        operation
      \[
        (\unitary \otimes \unitary[V])(\ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{\varphi})
          = (\unitary \otimes \unitary[V]) \ket{\psi, \varphi}\, .
      \]
      The unitary matrix formed by the tensor product 
        $\unitary \otimes \unitary[V]$
        will be a $4 \times 4$ unitary, 
        a two-qubit operation that combine the effects
        of both single-qubit unitaries in parallel,
        (that is, in the same time step).

      Following the register-subscript convention introduced earlier, if we wanted to
        apply $\unitary$ only to the first qubit, we would like to be able to write 
      \[
        \unitary_1 \ket{\psi, \varphi} = \unitary_1 \ket{\psi}_1 \ket{\varphi}_2\, ,
      \]
      which we may now define as
      \[
        \unitary_1 (\ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{\varphi})
        \coloneqq (\unitary \otimes \idmatrix)(\ket{\psi} \otimes
        \ket{\varphi})\, ,
      \]
      and likewise if we want to only apply $\unitary[V]$ to the second qubit:
      \[
        \unitary[V]_2 \ket{\psi, \varphi}
        \coloneqq (\idmatrix \otimes \unitary[V])(\ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{\varphi})\, .
      \]

      Extending to multi-qubit registers is straightforward: 
        Let $\ket{\psi} = \ket{\psi}_{\register[R],\register[S],\register[T]}$ 
        be defined over a number of qubits that can be
        split into three sub-registers $\register[R]$, $\register[S]$, and
        $\register[T]$, comprising $n$, $m$, and $k$ qubits, respectively.
        Say you have a $2^m \times 2^m$-unitary $\unitary$, and you would like to apply
        it to the qubits in register $\register[S]$. Then we may write:
      \[
        \unitary[U]_{\register[S]} \ket{\psi}
        \coloneqq (\idmatrix_{2^n \times 2^n} \otimes \unitary \otimes 
        \idmatrix_{2^k \times 2^k}) \ket{\psi}\, .
      \]

      In this sense, the tensor product represents combinations of operations
        happening at the same moment in time, but across different points in the
        \emph{space} of qubit registers.

      Now, imagine that you instead have two (single- or multi-qubit) unitaries
        $\unitary$ and $\unitary[V]$, and that you
        would like to first apply $\unitary$, then $\unitary[V]$. In other words, you want
        something to the effect of
      \[
        \ket{\psi} \apply{\unitary} \ket{\psi^\prime}
          \apply{\unitary[V]} \ket{\psi^{\prime\prime}}\, .
      \]
      This is given by the following product.
      \[
        \unitary[V] \unitary \ket{\psi}
        = \unitary[V] (\unitary \ket{\psi})
        = \unitary[V] \ket{\psi^\prime}
        = \ket{\psi^{\prime\prime}}\, .
      \]
      Hence, the matrix product represents operations happening in different
        moments in time, in this case across two time steps, with the right
        unitary applied first and the left one thereafter. 

      Note the reverse time-ordering! Given that most matrices don't commute,
        this order clearly matters, and the above operational interpretation
        tells us why: Two unitaries commute if and only if
        applying the operations in either order always yields the same result,
        regardless of state.

      But hold on -- is ``time step'' even a well-defined unit here? 
        After all, with $\unitary$ and $\unitary[V]$ being arbitrary unitary
        matrices, we could always define a new unitary $\unitary[W] = \unitary[V]
        \unitary$, and say that \emph{this} is the operation that was applied,
        now apparently in unit time!
        This is why before analysing the time it would take to run a quantum
        algorithm, we first have to agree on an \emph{elementary gate set}, so
        that we can take those to be unit-time, and let the algorithm we actually
        analyze be the implementation ``compiled'' to this gate set,
        %by applying those gates in series and parallel, implicitly 
        factoring the final unitary into tensor and matrix products of the
        elementary unitaries.
        More on this in \cref{sec:universality}, when we discuss
        \emph{complexity}.

      To summarize, the tensor product represents unitaries applied in
        \emph{parallel}, while the matrix product represents unitaries applied in
        \emph{series}.

  \subsection{Measurements}\label{sec:measurements}
    At the end of our quantum algorithm, we would usually like to read out
      some classical information by measuring the end-state. 
      We mentioned earlier that measurements
      constitute an exception to the rule that any operation performed on a
      quantum state must be unitary. We will give two explanations:
      First, the easily-applied intuition, then the more powerful mathematical
      formalism.

    \subsubsection{State Collapse.}
      If you perform a standard, computational-basis measurement on a state 
        $\ket \psi = \sum_x \alpha_x \ket x$, then you will observe value $x$ with
        probability $\abs{\alpha_x}^2 = \alpha_x^* \alpha_x$. After observation,
        if you continue to perform the same measurement on the state, you will continue to
        see the same value $x$ -- we say that the superposition state $\ket \psi$
        has \emph{collapsed} to the basis state $\ket x$. In other words, this
        post-collapse state has $\alpha_x = 1$, with all the other $\alpha$ set to
        $0$.
      \[
        \ket{\psi} = \sum_x \alpha_x \ket x\ \apply{\observe}\ \ket{\psi^\prime} =
        \ket x\, .
      \]
      Note the irreversibility: Once the state
        collapses, any information about the prior state is \emph{deleted},
        except for the single bit of information
        that $\alpha_x$ had non-zero magnitude. 

      That is really all you need to know about measurements to get started with
        quantum computing.
        But if you'd like a peek at the machinery required to place measurements
        on formal ground, read on -- for if observing a quantum state is not a
        unitary operation, then what kind of operation \emph{is} it?

      \begin{exercise}

        where $\theta, \phi \in [0,2\pi)$ are \emph{phases}.

            \]
            where $\theta^\prime$ is the \emph{relative phase} between $\ket 0$

    \subsubsection{Projectors.} 
      To see what kind of non-unitary matrix is needed to represent the act of
        measuring a state, recall that after state collapse, further
        observations is guaranteed to just yield the same result.
        %(using the same measurement basis, to be defined shortly), 
        %then the outcome will be the same.
        The state has already collapsed; it cannot collapse
        \emph{further}.\footnote{
          Unless you switch your measurement basis, that is; more on this in a minute.
        }

      Let $\projector$ represent such an \emph{idempotent} operation. Then,
        \[ 
          \projector (\projector \ket{\psi}) =
          %\projector^2 \ket{\psi} 
          \projector \ket{\psi}
          \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 
          \projector^2 = \projector\, .
        \]

      \noindent
      Operators like $\projector$ are known as \emph{projectors}, so named because they 
        effectively project higher dimensional objects onto some lower-dimensional
        hyperplane.
        %---or, in the case of measuring all the qubits at once,
        %all the way down to one of the basis vectors.
        For single-qubit states, it is easy to verify that the outer product 
        of a basis state with itself will satisfy the definition: Let
        $\projector_0$ and $\projector_1$ be given by
      \[
        \projector_0 = \ketbra{0}
        = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
        = \begin{sqmatrix} 
          1 & 0 \\ 
          0 & 0 
        \end{sqmatrix}\, , \quad
        \projector_1 \coloneqq \ketbra{1}
        = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
        = \begin{sqmatrix} 
          0 & 0 \\ 
          0 & 1 
        \end{sqmatrix}\, . \quad
      \]
      From these matrix representations, we can see that effect of applying
        $\projector_b$ to a single-qubit state is to single out the
        $\ket b$-part of the state from the superposition. For example, applying
        $\projector_0$ to a general qubit state yields:
      \[
        \projector_0 \ket{\psi} = \projector_0(\alpha \ket{0} + \beta \ket{1})
        = \begin{sqmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{sqmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_0 \\ \alpha_1 \end{bmatrix} 
        = \alpha_0 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \alpha_0 \ket{0}\, .
      \]
      Applying $\projector_0$ a second time, then, has no effect:
      \[
        \projector_0^2 \ket{\psi} = \projector_0 (\alpha_0 \ket{0})
        = \begin{sqmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{sqmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} 
          %= \alpha_0 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} 
          = \alpha_0 \ket{0} = \projector_0 \ket{\psi}\, .
      \]
      If we instead apply $\projector_1$ as the second projector, we get the zero vector:
      \[
        \projector_1 \projector_0 \ket{\psi} = \projector_1 (\alpha_0 \ket{0})
        = \begin{sqmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{sqmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} 
          = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\, .
      \]
      Hence, these projectors are \emph{orthogonal} in the sense that they
        satisfy $\projector_i \projector_j = \delta_{ij} \projector_i$.
        What's more, the set $\{ \projector_0, \projector_1 \}$ is \emph{complete}
        in the sense that it sums to identity:
      \[
        \projector_0 + \projector_1 = 
        \begin{sqmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{sqmatrix}
        + \begin{sqmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{sqmatrix}
        = \begin{sqmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{sqmatrix} = \idmatrix\, .
      \]
      On the other hand, these matrices are manifestly \emph{not} unitary, as
        can be seen from the fact that they are not norm-preserving (cf.\
        \cref{ex:unitarity}): If $\ket\psi$ starts out as a
        superposition state, then $\projector_0 \ket\psi$ will not be
        unit-length and therefore not a valid quantum state. 
        Alternatively, you may have noticed that the projector
        matrices are not even \emph{invertible}, 
        reflecting the information loss incurred by measurements.
        %after measuring a state, there is no way to recover the full pre-collapse
        %state, other than to re-create it from scratch!

      \hhnote{
        We also need Hermiticity if we want our probabilities to end up
        positive; make counterexample an exercise?
      }

      \begin{definition}[Positive Semi-Definite (PSD), Positive Definite (PD)]
        Let $\Hspace$ be a Hilbert space, and let $\unitary[A]$ be a Hermitian
        operator on $\Hspace$ ($\unitary[A]^\dagger = \unitary[A]$).
        Then $\unitary[A]$ is \emph{positive semi-definite}, written
        $\unitary[A] \succeq 0$, if for every pure state $\ket\psi \in \Hspace$,
        \[
          \braket{\psi|\unitary[A]|\psi} \geq 0\, .
        \]
        If the inequality is strict, then $\unitary[A]$ is
        \emph{positive definite} (PD), written $\unitary[A] \succ 0$.
      \end{definition}

      \begin{definition}[Complete, Orthogonal Projectors]
        A set of $k$ projectors $\{ \projector_{i} \}_{i \in [k]}$ on a
          Hilbert space $\Hspace$ is said to be
          \emph{complete} if the projectors sum to identity:
          \[
            \sum_{i \in [k]} \projector_i = \idmatrix\, ,
          \]
          and \emph{orthogonal} if the projectors are
          \emph{Hermitian}, \emph{idempotent}, and \emph{mutually orthogonal}:
          \[
            \projector_i^\dagger = \projector_i\, ,\qquad
            \projector_i \projector_j = \delta_{ij}\projector_i\, .
          \]
          Then the projectors $\projector_i$ are PSD operators with eigenvalues in $\bin$ (as follows
          from $\projector_i = \projector_i^\dagger$ and $\projector_i^2 =
          \projector_i$) which project $\Hspace$ onto mutually orthogonal
          subspaces $\Sspace_i \coloneqq \projector_i \Hspace$ that together compose the full Hilbert space
          (as follows from orthogonality and completeness): 
          \[
            \bigoplus_{i\in[k]} \Sspace_i = \Hspace\, .
          \]
      \end{definition}


    \subsubsection{Projective Measurements.}
      We already mentioned the Born rule, which says that the probability of
        observing an outcome is equal to the norm-squared of the corresponding
        amplitude; hence, quantum states (which are vectors of amplitudes) are the quantum
        equivalent of probability distributions (lists of probabilities). 
        For example, performing a computational-basis measurement on a qubit in
        the state $\ket\psi = \alpha_0 \ket0 + \alpha_1 \ket1$ induces the
        distribution
        \[
          \distribution_\psi^{\computational} 
          = \left(\abs{\alpha_0}^2, \abs{\alpha_1}^2\right)
          \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \distribution_\psi^{\computational}(b) 
          = \abs{\alpha_b}^2 = \norm{\projector_b \ket\psi}^2 
          = \braket{\psi|\projector_b|\psi}\, ,
        \]
      \hhnote{
        kinda feels like we have no choice but to count list
        elements from $0$ instead of $1$
      }
      where we used the properties of the projectors $\projector_0,
      \projector_1$ to rewrite the probability of observing outcome $b$.
      This suggests a natural $n$-qubit generalization: 
      Let $\ket\psi = \sum_x \alpha_x \ket{x}$. Then performing a
      computational-basis measurement on all $n$ qubits induces the distribution
      \[
        \distribution_\psi^{\computational}(x) 
        = \abs{\alpha_x}^2 = \braket{\psi|\projector_x|\psi}\, .
      \]

      What about other measurements that you might want to perform? 
        Say, for example, you only want to measure whether the first bit $x_0$ of
        the string $x$ is equal to $0$ or $1$. To express this as a measurement on
        $\ket\psi$, you might define the projectors
      \[
        \projector_{x_0 = 0} \coloneqq \projector_0 \otimes \idmatrix_{2^{n-1}}\, ,
        \quad
        \projector_{x_0 = 1} \coloneqq \projector_1 \otimes \idmatrix_{2^{n-1}}\, .
      \]
      Then $\{\projector_{x_0 = 0}, \projector_{x_0 = 1}\}$ is a complete set of
        orthogonal projectors, as they must be, since they each correspond to
        mutually-exclusive outcomes whose probabilities sum to one. 
        Call the set of these two projectors $\measurement_{x_0}$. 
        Performing the corresponding measurement then induces the distribution
      \[
        \distribution_\psi^{\measurement_{x_0}}(b) 
        = \braket{\psi|\projector_{x_0 = b}|\psi}
        = \sum_{x\, :\, x_0 = b} \abs{\alpha_x}^2\, .
      \]

      The set $\measurement_{x_0}$ is an example of a \emph{Projector-Valued
        Measure} (PVM). Any set of complete, orthogonal projectors defines a
        PVM; conversely, for any \emph{sharp} measurement (meaning measurements
        such that repeating them yields the same outcome) such that there are $k$
        possible, mutually exclusive outcomes, there is a corresponding
        PVM.\footnote{
          The more general class of measurements are the POVMs, or ``Positive
          Operator-Valued Measures'', which capture the class of measurements
          with $k$ possible, mutually exclusive outcomes (like above), but
          which may be \emph{non-sharp}, meaning that repeating the measurement
          may yield a different results. 
          (Intuitively, you may think of POVMs as being to PVMs what mixed
          states are to pure states.)
        } We call such measurements \emph{projective}.

      \begin{definition}[Projective Measurement]\label{def:pvm}
        Let $\{ \projector_{i} \}_{i \in [k]}$ be a complete,
          orthogonal set of projectors on a Hilbert space $\Hspace$.
          Then $\measurement = \{\projector_{i} \}_{i \in [k]}$ is a 
          \emph{Projection-Valued Measure (PVM)}, under which measuring a
          state $\ket{\psi} \in \Hspace$
          %yields outcome $i$ with probability
          induces a probability distribution
          $\distribution_{\psi}^{\measurement}$ over outcomes 
          $i \in [k]$, given by
          \[
            \distribution_\psi^{\measurement}(i) 
            \coloneqq \norm{\projector_i \ket\psi}^2
            = \braket{\psi|\projector_i|\psi}\, .
          \]
          %and, conditioned on outcome $i$, the post-measurement state will be
          %\[
          %  \ket{\psi^\prime} = 
          %  \frac{\projector_{i}\ket{\psi}}{\norm{\projector_{i} \ket{\psi}}}\, .
          %\]
          We will use $X \gets \measurement(\ket\psi)$
          as shorthand for sampling a random variable $X$ from the distribution
          $\distribution_\psi^{\measurement}$.
      \end{definition}

      The projected state $\projector_i \ket{\psi}$ will not (usually) be
        unit-norm, and hence not a valid quantum state, so we still need to
        define an update procedure for the post-measurement state, given that a
        particular outcome $i$ was observed: If we observed outcome $i$, 
        then the projected state vector $\projector_i \ket{\psi}$ will be
        pointing in the right direction in Hilbert space, but its magnitude will be 
        $\norm{\projector_i \ket{\psi}} = \abs{\alpha_i} \leq 1$. 
        Physically speaking, observing a quantum state has the
        effect of ``snapping'' the vector from the old direction in Hilbert
        space, to a new direction consistent with the observed outcome -- the
        magnitude never shifts away from $1$. Mathematically, we can recover this 
        behaviour by defining the post-measurement state to be the projected state vector
        $\projector_i \ket{\psi}$ (giving the direction), \emph{renormalized}
        to $1$ by dividing away the factor $\abs{\alpha_i}$.

      \begin{definition}[Post-Measurement State]
        Let $\measurement = \{\projector_{i} \}_{i \in [k]}$ be a PVM.
        Upon observing a state $\ket\psi$, if outcome $i$ is observed,
        the state will immediately\footnote{
          Whether the state update upon measuring is truly
          instantaneous, or if there is a continuous, dynamical
          process underlying it (or if the question is ill-posed), 
          remains a hotly debated question in the foundations of physics.
        }
        update (or ``collapse'') to the state 
        \[
          \ket{\psi} \apply{\measurement \to i} 
          \mathsf{Collapse}^{\measurement}_i(\ket{\psi}) 
          \coloneqq \frac{\projector_i \ket\psi}{\norm{\projector_i \ket{\psi}}}
          = \frac{\projector_i \ket\psi}{\sqrt{\braket{\psi|\projector_i|\psi}}}\, ,
        \]
        where conditioning on outcome $i$ guarantees $\prob{\text{observe } i\, } > 0 \To \norm{\projector_i \ket{\psi}} > 0$.
      \end{definition}
      Note how $\mathsf{Collapse}^{\measurement}_i(\ket{\psi})$ is 
        a \emph{non-linear} map on the Hilbert space, in stark contrast to the
        unitary operations we have encountered so far.
        In fact, $\mathsf{Collapse}^{\measurement}_i(\ket{\psi})$ is 
        not even injective -- there is an infinite continuity of
        pre-measurement states that could have yielded the
        post-measurement state -- which shows how measurements
        are fundamentally \emph{irreversible operations}.

          \item Show that the set $\measurement_{x_0} = \{\projector_{x_0 = 0}, \projector_{x_0 = 1}\}$
            is complete and orthogonal.
          %\item Show that if $\ket\psi = \sum_x \alpha_x \ket x$, then
          %  $\distribution_\psi^{\measurement_{x_0}}(b) = \sum_{x\, :\, x_0 = b}
          %  \abs{\alpha_x}^2$.
          \item Show that the projector $\projector_{x_0=b}$ is equal to the sum

    \subsubsection{Distinguishing Between Quantum States.}
      \newcommand{\unknown}{\ket{\emph{?}\, }} % local macro
      Say that you are given qubit register $\register$ in some unknown state $\unknown$,
        but you are promised that $\unknown$ is equal to one of two known states (which
        you also know how to prepare), $\ket\psi$ or $\ket\varphi$. What
        measurement should you do to maximize your likelihood of telling them
        apart? Note that $\ket\psi$ and $\ket\varphi$ need not be orthogonal, so
        there may be many possible measurement outcomes that are consistent with
        $\unknown$ being either state.

      \paragraph{The SWAP Test.}
        A natural first thing to try is the procedure known as the ``SWAP test'':
          Prepare a new register $\register[S]$ in one of the two states, say
          $\ket \psi$, and prepare an ancilla qubit $\qubit$ in the uniform
          superposition $\ket +$. 
          Then, perform a controlled SWAP of the states $\unknown$ and
          $\ket\psi$, controlled on the ancilla qubit:
        \[
          \ket{+}_\qubit
          \unknown_{\register}
          \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
          =
          \left( \frac{\ket{0}_\qubit + \ket{1}_\qubit}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          \unknown_{\register}
          \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
          \apply{\CSWAPgate} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}
          \left(
            \ket{0}_\qubit 
            \unknown_{\register}
            \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
          + \ket{1}_\qubit
            \ket{\psi}_{\register}
            \unknown_{\register[S]}
          \right)\, .
        \]
        Then, apply a Hadamard gate to $\qubit$ and perform a computational-basis
          measurement on it. If the unknown
          state is $\ket\psi$, then the $\CSWAPgate$ operation had no effect:
        \[
          \left( \frac{\ket{0}_\qubit + \ket{1}_\qubit}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          \ket{\psi}_{\register}
          \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
          \apply{\CSWAPgate} 
          \left( \frac{\ket{0}_\qubit + \ket{1}_\qubit}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          \ket{\psi}_{\register}
          \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
          \apply{\Hgate}
          \ket{0}_\qubit
          \ket{\psi}_{\register}
          \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}\, ,
        \]
        so that when we measure $\qubit$, we are guaranteed to observe the value
          $0$. Hence, if we observe a $1$, then we learn that $\unknown$ is \emph{not}
          identical to $\ket\psi$, from which we may conclude that $\unknown$ must
          be $\ket\varphi$. 

        What is the probability that we observe $1$ in this case? 
          If $\unknown$ is equal to $\ket\varphi$, then we get
        \begin{multline*}
          \left( \frac{\ket{0}_\qubit + \ket{1}_\qubit}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
          \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
          \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
          \apply{\CSWAPgate} 
          \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}
          \left(
            \ket{0}_\qubit 
            \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
            \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
          + \ket{1}_\qubit
            \ket{\psi}_{\register}
            \ket{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
          \right) \\
          %
          \apply{\Hgate}
          \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}
          \left(
            \left(
              \frac{
                \ket{0}_\qubit 
                + \ket{1}_\qubit 
              }{\sqrt 2}
            \right)
            \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
            \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
          + \left(
              \frac{
                \ket{0}_\qubit 
                - \ket{1}_\qubit 
              }{\sqrt 2}
            \right)
            \ket{\psi}_{\register}
            \ket{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
          \right) \\
          = \frac{1}{2}
          \left(
            \ket{0} 
            \left(
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
              \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
              +
              \ket{\psi}_{\register}
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
            \right)
            + \ket{1} 
            \left(
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
              \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
              -
              \ket{\psi}_{\register}
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
            \right)
          \right)\, .
        \end{multline*}
        Let $\projector^{(\qubit)}_{b}$ be the projector $\projector_b \otimes
          \idmatrix_{\register} \otimes \idmatrix_{\register[S]}$, i.e.\ the projector
          that acts like $\projector_b$ on $\qubit$ and like the identity on 
          $\register$ and $\register[S]$.
          Then, \cref{def:pvm} tells us that the probability that measuring
          $\qubit$ will yield outcome $1$ is
        \begin{multline*}
          %\condprob{\text{outcome } 1}{\unknown = \ket{\varphi}} = 
          \norm{\projector^{(\qubit)}_1 
          \frac{1}{2}
          \left(
            \ket{0} 
            \left(
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
              \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
              +
              \ket{\psi}_{\register}
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
            \right)
            + \ket{1} 
            \left(
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
              \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
              -
              \ket{\psi}_{\register}
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
            \right)
          \right)
          }^2 \\
          %
          =
          \norm{
          \frac{1}{2}
            \ket{1} 
            \left(
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
              \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
              -
              \ket{\psi}_{\register}
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
            \right)
          }^2 
          = 
          \frac{1}{4}
          \abs{
            \braket{1|1}
            \left(
              \bra{\psi}_{\register[S]}
              \bra{\varphi}_{\register}
              -
              \bra{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
              \bra{\psi}_{\register}
            \right)
            \left(
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register}
              \ket{\psi}_{\register[S]}
              -
              \ket{\psi}_{\register}
              \ket{\varphi}_{\register[S]}
            \right) 
          } \\
          %
          \frac{1}{4}
          \abs{
            \braket{\varphi|\varphi}_{\register}
            \braket{\psi|\psi}_{\register[S]}
            +
            \braket{\psi|\psi}_{\register}
            \braket{\varphi|\varphi}_{\register[S]}
            -
            \braket{\psi|\varphi}_{\register}
            \braket{\varphi|\psi}_{\register[S]}
            -
            \braket{\varphi|\psi}_{\register}
            \braket{\psi|\varphi}_{\register[S]}
          }
          = 
          \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} 
          \abs{
            \braket{\psi|\varphi}
          }^2\, .
          %
        \end{multline*}
        Note how, even if $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\varphi}$ are orthogonal, then the
          probability of observing $1$ is only $\frac{1}{2} - 0 = \frac{1}{2}$, so
          that even in the best case, while observing a $1$ would allow us to
          immediately conclude that $\unknown = \ket\varphi$, observing the
          zero outcome would only provide relatively weak evidence towards
          $\unknown$ being $\ket\psi$, achieving in expectation the success rate
        \[
          \expect{\, \text{success}\, } = \frac{1}{2} \prob{0 \gets \Helstrom(\ket{\psi})} 
            + \frac{1}{2} \prob{1 \gets \Helstrom(\ket{\varphi})}
            = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}
            \left(
              \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} 
              \abs{
                \braket{\psi|\varphi}
              }^2
            \right) = \frac{3}{4} - \frac{1}{4} \abs{\braket{\psi|\varphi}}^2\, .
        \]
        As a sanity check, notice how the expected success rate
          falls to $\half$ when the states are identical, and how orthogonal
          states maximize the rate.

        Given several copies of $\unknown$, we
          could of course amplify our distinguishing advantage by repeating the
          experiment. But what if we only had the one copy of $\unknown$?
          With the state being unknown to us, the \emph{no-cloning theorem}
          (see \cref{sec:entanglement})
          bars us from generating copies of it, and we are stuck with a
          single measurement. Can we do better?

      \paragraph{The Helstrom Measurement.}
        As it turns out, the answer is yes, and the \emph{optimal} distinguishing
          measurement is known as the \emph{Helstrom measurement} after its
          discoverer.
          %
        \begin{theorem}[The Helstrom Measurement]\label{thm:helstrom}
          Given a register $\register$ promised to be in either state
          $\ket\psi$ or state $\ket\varphi$ with equal probability, 
          then the Helstrom measurement $\Helstrom$ 
          correctly identifies the state with probability
          \[
            \prob{0 \gets \Helstrom(\ket{\psi})} 
            = 
            \prob{1 \gets \Helstrom(\ket{\varphi})} 
            = 
            \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{1 - \abs{\braket{\psi|\varphi}}^2}\, ,
          \]
          which is optimal. 
        \end{theorem}
        %
        Unlike our SWAP test, if the two possible states $\ket\psi$, $\ket\varphi$
          are orthogonal, then the Helstrom measurement is
          guaranteed to distinguish them:
          \[
            \braket{\psi|\varphi} = 0 \implies
            \prob{0 \gets \Helstrom(\ket{\psi})} 
            = 
            \prob{1 \gets \Helstrom(\ket{\varphi})} 
            = 
            \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{1 - 0} = 1\, .
          \]
          In that case, the measurement is simply given by the PVM
          \[
            \Helstrom = \{ \projector_\psi, \projector_\varphi \}
            = \{ \ketbra{\psi}, \ketbra{\varphi} \}\, .
          \]
          
        % local macros
        \newcommand{\orthpsi}{\tilde{\psi}}
        \newcommand{\orthphi}{\tilde{\varphi}}
        What about the more general case?
          Well, if $\ket\psi$ and $\ket\varphi$ are non-orthogonal,
          then $\{ \ketbra{\psi}, \ketbra{\varphi} \}$ is not a
          PVM, since these projectors will not orthogonal either -- we need to build
          two \emph{orthogonal} projectors from $\ket\psi$ and $\ket\varphi$.
          The Helstrom measurement does this by picking out
          the optimal orientation in the 2D plane 
          spanned by $\ket\psi$ and $\ket{\varphi}$, 
          as illustrated in \cref{fig:helstrom}:
          Let $\ket{\orthpsi}$ and $\ket{\orthphi}$ be the
          two orthogonal states that 1) sit in the plane spanned by $\ket\psi$
          and $\ket\varphi$, and 2) are oriented such that the bisecting
          line between $\ket\psi$ and $\ket\varphi$ sits at a $45^\circ$
          angle to both states. Then
          the Helstrom measurement is given by the PVM
          \[
            \Helstrom = \{ \projector_\psi, \projector_\varphi \}
            = \{ \ketbra{\orthpsi}, \ketbra{\orthphi} \}\, ,
          \]
          which optimally distinguishes the two states, provided either 
          state were given to us with equal probability.\footnote{
            There is also the \emph{weighted} Helstrom measurement, for the case
            that the probability of receiving each state is shifted away from
            $\half$ by some known amount. If the probability of receiving each

            effectively biases the measurement by rotating
            the measurement axes $\ket{\orthpsi}$ and $\ket{\orthphi}$ in
            \cref{fig:helstrom} by the angle (which can be derived from $p$) that maximizes
            the expected success rate, achieving the optimal rate
            \[
              \expect{\, \text{success}\, } = p \prob{0 \gets \Helstrom(\ket{\psi})} 
                + (1-p) \prob{1 \gets \Helstrom(\ket{\varphi})}
                = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{1 - 4 p (1-p)
                \abs{\braket{\psi|\varphi}}^2}\, .
            \]
            %which can again be shown to be optimal.
          }

  \subsection{Entanglement}\label{sec:entanglement}
    Just like classical probabilities can be correlated, so can quantum states
      be \emph{entangled}. Consider the following, two-qubit product state:
    \[
      \ket{\psi} = \ket + \ket + 
      = \left( \frac{
        \ket{0} + \ket{1}
      }{\sqrt 2}\right )
      \left( \frac{
        \ket{0} + \ket{1}
      }{\sqrt 2}\right )
      = \frac{
        \ket{00} + \ket{01} + \ket{10} + \ket{11}
      }{2}\, .
    \]
    Regardless of which measurement you apply to $\ket \psi$, the value
      observed in the first qubit will bear no implication for the value 
      that the second qubit will take. We say
      that the state is \emph{separable}, which is to say that 
      any measurement on $\ket\psi$ will induce a \emph{product distribution} along the
      same separating line.

    Contrast with the following two-qubit state:
    \[
      \ket\varphi 
      = \frac{
        \ket{00} + \ket{11}
      }{\sqrt 2}\, .
    \]
    With the middle $\ket{01}$ and $\ket{10}$ missing, there is no way to write
      this state as a product of two single-qubit states; the state is
      \emph{non-separable}, which is to say that observing the state will yield
      outcome distributions with correlations between the values of the two
      qubits. In this case it is easy to see, as the two qubits are perfectly
      correlated: If you perform a computational-basis measurement, you will see
      the string $00$ or the string $11$, never $01$ or $10$. Hence, if you
      observe the first qubit and see $1$, you \emph{know} that observing the
      second qubit will also yield the value $1$.

    This is the phenomenon known as quantum \emph{entanglement}, and despite its
      reputation as something deeply mysterious, it really is
      just the quantum version of correlated probability distributions.
      The apparent mysteriousness comes from the fact that the way we 
      formalized measurements in \cref{sec:measurements}, observing one of the
      qubits of the state $\ket\varphi$ above leads to collapse of the full
      state, so that the second qubit -- which previously was in a superposition
      of $0$ and $1$ -- is suddenly fully determined. The immediateness of this
      effect means the state collapse will not even respect the light-speed
      limit, so that Alice measuring a qubit on Earth will immediately collapse the
      superposition of Bob's qubit, even if Bob finds himself at the opposite side of the
      galaxy. This insight famously lead to Einstein's lament that quantum theory
      contained ``spooky action at a distance''\footnote{\emph{Spukhafte
      Fernwirkung} in the original German.}, and that it must therefore be
      fundamentally flawed.

    \subsubsection{Entanglement vs.\ Randomness.}
    The modern view is that the above is taking the pure-state formalism too
      literally: The actual state that Bob would receive upon receiving the
      second qubit would be a \emph{maximally mixed state}, which is to say a
      state that is perfectly consistent with a classical coin-flip.
      %the qubit in the $\ket 0$ or $\ket 1$ state with 50/50 probability; 
      The only way to distinguish the entangled state from a random one would be
      to bring the two qubits back together, which by assumption is beyond Bob's
      capability. With this mixed-state formalism in hand, 
      it becomes clear that measurements applied to the qubit on Earth are
      completely irrelevant to the statistics observed by Bob at the other end of the
      galaxy. 

    We will work out the details of this thought experiment when we later
      introduce the density-matrix formulation, but for now, the take-away message
      is one that will turn out to be central to the compressed-oracle
      technique\iffutureversion, covered in \cref{sec:compressed}\fi:
    %
    \begin{center}
      \emph{Inaccessible entanglement is perfectly indistinguishable from classical randomness.}
    \end{center}
    %
    \subsubsection{Entanglement the Resource.}
    All this is not to undersell the \emph{power} of entanglement, as it is one of
      the central resources of quantum algorithms and interactive quantum
      protocols alike! Indeed, the word ``resource'' is justified by the fact that
      entanglement is a \emph{basis-independent}, and
      hence somehow ``objective'', property of the quantum state: 
      the presence of correlations between different parts of the quantum system
      does not depend on which measurement you choose to apply to the state.

    \begin{exercise}
      \begin{subex}
        \item Show that measuring $\ket\varphi$ 
          under a computational-basis and a Hadamard-basis
          measurements lead to the same outcome statistics by showing the identity
          \[
            \ket{\varphi} = \frac{ \ket{00} + \ket{11} }{\sqrt 2} = 
            \frac{ \ket{++} + \ket{--} }{\sqrt 2}\, .
          \]
        \item Entanglement may be measurement-independent, but outcome
          statistics may not be: Let
          \[
            \ket{\xi} = \frac{ \ket{00} - \ket{11} }{\sqrt 2}\, .
          \]
          What are the distributions induced by performing a computational-basis
          versus a Hadamard-basis measurement on $\ket \xi$?
      \end{subex}
    \end{exercise}

    \subsubsection{No Cloning.}
      However, entanglement can in practice be as much a hinderance to quantum
        algorithm design as a resource. The
        simplest example of this is perhaps the case of copying, or ``cloning'',
        quantum states. Say you are given a qubit in some unknown superposition state
      \[
        \ket{\psi} = \alpha\ket 0 + \beta\ket 1\, .
      \]
      Naturally, you might want to create a copy of it, i.e.\ to create the
      state
      \[
        \ket{\psi}\ket{\psi} = 
        \left(
          \alpha\ket 0 + \beta\ket 1
        \right)
        \left(
          \alpha\ket 0 + \beta\ket 1
        \right)
        = \alpha^2 \ket{00} + \alpha\beta \ket{01} + \alpha\beta \ket{10} +
        \beta^2 \ket{11}\, .
      \]
      The first thing to try would be to prepare a second qubit in the $\ket 0$
        state, then apply the controlled-not operation from the first to the
        second qubit, which will flip the qubit to $\ket 1$ exactly in the $\beta$
        branch of the original state. The result is: 
      \[
        \ket{\psi}\ket{0} = 
        \left(
          \alpha\ket 0 + \beta\ket 1
        \right)
        \ket 0 \apply{\CXgate} 
        \alpha\ket{00} + \beta\ket{11}
        \neq 
        \ket{\psi}\ket{\psi}\, . 
      \]
      Rather than a product of two copies of the original state, we have ended
      up with a maximally entangled state, fundamentally different from the
      superposition state we started with! If only we could somehow remove the
      entanglement by \emph{inserting} the missing
      $\ket{01}$ and $\ket{10}$ terms, appropriately scaled, then perhaps we
      could get to the desired product state. 

    Alas, this turns out to be a fundamental constraint
      of superposition states, known today as the \emph{no-cloning theorem}.

      \begin{exercise}
        In this exercise you will prove the no-cloning theorem.
          Let $\unitary$ be a two-qubit unitary operation that
          clones the single-qubit state $\ket\psi$ into an initialized qubit state,
          as follows:
          \[
            \unitary \ket\psi \ket 0 = \ket\psi \ket\psi\, .
          \]
          The no-cloning theorem then states that no single unitary
          $\unitary$ can clone several arbitrary qubit states.\footnote{
            Trivially, there exists a unitary $\unitary$ that clones
            $\ket{\psi}$ \emph{specifically}, namely the unitary implemented by
            whatever circuit built $\ket\psi$ in the first place.
          }
        \begin{subex}
          \item Let $\ket\varphi$ be a second state, also cloneable via 
            $\unitary$ in the same manner as above. 
            Prove the theorem by deriving a restriction on the possible values
            that the inner product $\braket{\psi|\varphi}$ can take.
          \item The previous result showed that, while cloning is not possible 
            in general, some qubit states \emph{can} be cloned.
            What are these ``clonable'' states? How should they be interpreted?
        \end{subex}
      \end{exercise}

  \subsection{Universality}\label{sec:universality}
    %We are concerned with (protecting against) quantum algorithms, and so, as
    %  with any algorithm, we need some way of measuring its resources, i.e.\ its
    %  \emph{time} and \emph{space} (number of qubits) requirements.
    %
    %\subsubsection{Measuring Time and Space.} 
      Earlier, we argued that the matrix product of unitaries represents
        operations happening in series, i.e.\ at different time steps. 
        While true, this does not give a well-defined measure of time, as 
        the product of two unitaries is just another unitary, which begs the
        question: did the operation happen in two time-steps, or one?

      This is why, just like for classical circuits, we need to choose a
        set of \emph{elementary quantum gates}. Then, time can be measured by
        counting how many of the elementary gates must be run \emph{in series}
        in order to implement your unitary; this is known as the circuit
        \emph{depth}, corresponding to the algorithm's time complexity. 
        Gates that can be run in parallel need not count against
        the circuit's runtime, and the amount of parallelization your circuit
        employs at its highest is known as the circuit \emph{width}. 
        The circuit width tells you how much space (how many qubits) your
        circuit needs (sometimes referred to as the algorithm's
        space complexity).

    \subsubsection{Universal Quantum Gate Sets.}
      Of course, in order for your set of elementary gates to be able to implement
        \emph{any} unitary, it better be \emph{universal}.
        Classically, we know that any Boolean circuit can be generated by the
        AND, OR and NOT gates. However, on quantum computers we need all our
        operations (sans measurements) to be unitary, which at minimum means
        reversible (represented by invertible matrices). The AND and OR
        operations take two inputs and return one -- they delete information,
        and they are therefore \emph{not} reversible. Is this avoidable?

      Years before he started thinking about quantum protocols, Charles
        Bennett\footnote{
          Charles Bennett is the father not only of reversible computing, but
          also a founding father of quantum cryptography, having discovered 
          Quantum Key-Distribution (QKD) together with Gilles Brassard in 1984,
          today known as the BB84 protocol~\cite{BenBra84}. In 1993, the pair (together with
          several coauthors) also discovered the deeply fundamental and
          surprising fact that entanglement enables 
          ``teleporting'' quantum states across space using only classical
          communication~\cite{BBCJPW93}. In honour of their discoveries, Bennett
          and Brassard were awarded the 2025 Turing Award~\cite{ACMTuring25}.
        } was interested in reversible computing. He showed that, yes, 
        with a little overhead, any Boolean
        circuit can be made reversible~\cite{Bennett73}. As a small example, say you have a
        non-injective function $f$, implemented as an irreversible circuit, and
        you want to make it reversible. Then you may instead compute
        (using quantum notation in anticipation of our intended application):
        \[
          \ket{x}\ket{y} \to \ket{x}\ket{y \xor f(x)}\, .
        \]

      As it turns out, the classical three-bit \emph{Toffoli} gate $\CCXgate$
        (controlled-controlled-not: it flips the third bit iff the first two
        are both $1$) suffices for universality: any reversible circuit (which,
        remember, can also implement any \emph{irreversible} circuit) can be
        built from a series of Toffoli gates~\cite{Toffoli80}. In other words, for
        classical reversible computing, a
        universal elementary gate set is simply $\{ \CCXgate \}$.\footnote{
          The old, irreversible Boolean circuits had a universal gate set with
          only two-bit gates, namely AND, OR and NOT. Is there a universal gate set for
          (classical) reversible computing that only contains two-bit gates? The
          answer turns out to be \emph{no}: Toffoli showed that given only one-
          and two-bit gates, you could at most simulate \emph{linear} Boolean
          circuits; simulating non-linear circuits, including products, requires
          at least one three-bit gate~\cite{Toffoli80}.
          Surprisingly, as we will shortly see, this is no longer true for
          quantum circuits, where quantum universality \emph{can} be reached with
          only one- and two-qubit gates -- which therefore also achieves classical
          reversible universality!
          %despite being a strict generalizations reversible circuits!
        } We may then measure the time and space required by your circuit by
        first compiling it down to a Toffoli-only circuit, 
        and then count the number of Toffolis the resulting circuit applies in
        series vs.\ in parallel. 

      So that covers the classical case. What do we need to get to universal
        quantum computing? The answer is quite satisfactory: Take the classical universal gate
        set $\{\mathsf{Tof}\}$, and add the ability to create quantum superpositions
        via the Hadamard gate $\Hgate$; the quantum gate set $\{\CCXgate, \Hgate\}$
        is universal for quantum computers~\cite{Aharonov03}, and we may count time and space
        exactly like before.\footnote{
          Strictly speaking, starting from a real-valued initial state, 
          the gate set $\{\CCXgate, \Hgate\}$ can only reach other real-valued states,
          since $\CCXgate$ and $\Hgate$ are both real-valued matrices:
          states like $(\ket{0} + i \ket{1})/\sqrt 2$ will be forever out of reach.
          However, Bernstein and Vazirani showed that any $n$-qubit quantum circuit can be
          transformed into a observationally-equivalent, $n+1$-qubit, real-values-only quantum
          circuit, where the extra ancilla effectively keeps track of whether a given           
          amplitude should be read as real or imaginary~\cite{BerVaz97}.
          %; here,
          %``equivalent'' refers to the probability distribution induced by
          %measuring the final state.
          The gate set $\{\CCXgate, \Hgate\}$ is therefore sometimes said to be
          ``computationally universal'', rather than strictly so.
        } In fact, with multi-qubit gates being much more
        resource-intensive in current quantum devices than single-qubit gates
        such as $\Hgate$,
        ``number of Toffolis'' has arisen as a standard measure of the
        depth of near-term quantum circuits.

    \subsubsection{The Magic of Quantum Computation.}
      Another universal quantum gate set one often comes across is\footnote{
        Beware a common confusion: The $\Tgate$ ($\sqrt{\Sgate}$) gate $\neq$ the Toffoli
        ($\CCXgate$) gate!
      }
      $\{\CXgate, \Hgate, \Sgate, \Tgate\}$, known as ``Clifford $+$ T'', the Clifford
        gates being $\{\CXgate, \Hgate, \Sgate\}$. Here, the $\Sgate$ and $\Tgate$
        gates are defined as
      \[
        \Sgate = \sqrt{\Zgate} = \begin{sqmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i
        \end{sqmatrix}\, , \qquad
        \Tgate = \sqrt{\Sgate} = \begin{sqmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i \frac{\pi}{4}} \end{sqmatrix}\, .
      \]
      As written, this set is slightly redundant, since one can implement the
        $\Sgate$ gate by applying the $\Tgate$ gate twice; hence, the gate set 
        $\{\CXgate, \Hgate, \Tgate\}$ would also be universal.\footnote{
          Observe: No three-qubit gates!
        }

      Could we get away with only the Clifford gate set, 
        $\{\CXgate, \Hgate, \Sgate\}$? This gate set certainly \emph{looks} like
        it has all the required ingredients: the CNOT gate gives us most of
        classical computation, the Hadamard gate gives us superpositions, 
        combining them gives us entanglement, and the $\Sgate$ gate gives us
        not only negative, but also complex phases. (Recall that the
        $\{\CCXgate, \Hgate\}$ gate set reached a kind of computational 
        universality even without any use of complex phases.)

      And yet this turns out to not be enough: Restricting to the Clifford gates
        restricts the unitaries we can construct to a subgroup of the full unitary
        group known as the Clifford group. Worse, a famous result known as the
        Gottesman--Knill theorem~\cite{Gottesman98}
        tells us that \emph{any unitary of the Clifford group can be efficiently
        simulated on a classical computer}. In other words, if the Clifford
        gates were all we had, then any super-polynomial quantum advantage would
        vanish!

      Adding the innocious-seeming $\Tgate = \sqrt{\Sgate}$ gate to the set
        flips this picture on its head:
        Suddenly, not only can the gate set escape the classically-simulatable Clifford
        group, but it reaches full universality, quantum advantage and all.
        This seems to indicate that there is something special about the
        $\Tgate$ gate, since, when compiling circuits down to the Clifford $+$
        $\Tgate$ gate set, if the circuit can be implemented without using any
        (or using very few)
        $\Tgate$ gates, then it can also be classically simulated;
        contrapositively, if it can \emph{not} be classically simulated, then
        the compiled circuit must make use of many $\Tgate$ gates!
        Hence, the ``T-ness'' of a quantum circuit says something about
        \emph{how far beyond classical simulation} the circuit is, indicating a
        possible measure for its quantum advantage (assuming, of course, that
        the compilation method down to elementary gates minimizes the number of
        $\Tgate$ gates used, hence why it is useful to include both $\Sgate$ and
        $\Tgate$ gates in the gate set).

      This measure has a name, and it is \emph{quantum magic}. Besides
        giving us a way to quantify exactly what we mean by ``quantum
        advantage'', it has been recognized as a quantum \emph{resource} on par
        with entanglement, central to developments in quantum error correction.

  \subsection{Complexity}\label{sec:complexity}
    When we compared classical and quantum computation above,
      we were actually touching upon the theory of quantum \emph{complexity}, 
      the framework needed to understand \emph{if} and \emph{how} quantum
      computers outperform classical ones. 
      You may be familiar with the classes P
      (\underline{P}olynomial time) and NP (\underline{N}on-deterministic
      \underline{P}olynomial time)
      from classical complexity theory,
      and the million-dollar question of their (in)equality~\cite{Cook00}. The
      corresponding quantum complexity classes are BQP (\underline{B}ounded
      \underline{Q}uantum \underline{P}olynomial time) and QMA
      (\underline{Q}uantum \underline{M}erlin--\underline{A}rthur)
      respectively, which come with their own foundational ``is BQP $=$ QMA?''
      question.
      In this view, asking ``is quantum better than classical?'' is
      \emph{really} asking ``is P $=$ BQP?''\footnote{
        Technically the question is ``is BPP $=$ BQP?'', with \underline{B}ounded
        \underline{P}robabilistic \underline{P}olynomial time being the class of
        problems solvable by classical randomized algorithms.
      }

    These classes also come with their own theories of completeness: just like
      there are NP-complete problems,
      there are QMA-complete problems, as well as BQP-complete problems.
      The latter is a particularly interesting class of problems, since if
      there is \emph{any} superpolynomial quantum advantage at all, then
      these problems \emph{must} be classically hard!
      In contrast, while Shor's algorithm shows that integer factorization is in BQP,
      it is not (known to be) BQP-complete, leaving the door still open for that
      classical factorization algorithm which has eluded mathematicians for
      centuries.

  \subsection{Further Reading}
    This concludes our brief tour of the theory of quantum computing. The 
      aim has been to introduce the notation and mathematical formalism that
      we will employ, to the level of being able to 
      follow arguments and perform simple computations on quantum states, 
      while hopefully dispelling a few common misconceptions along the way. 
      Of course, there is much this primer did not
      touch upon, including illustrations of quantum circuits, 
      rotation gates and the Bloch-sphere visualization of single-qubit states, 
      quantum algorithms such as Grover's and Shor's,
      quantum-classical mixedness and more. If you want the full tour,
      Nielsen and Chuang's book \emph{Quantum Computation and
      Quantum Information}~\cite{NieChu10} is \emph{the} standard
      reference (and deservedly so!), although the mathematically-minded among you
      may prefer the introductory chapters of Mark Wilde's \emph{Quantum Information
      Theory}~\cite{Wilde17}, which treats the subject with a higher level of
      rigor than Nielsen and Chuang that still does not sacrifice the helpful
      intuitions.

    Needless to say, there is also a lot more to be said about computational complexity than
      the brief remarks given in \cref{sec:complexity}. Complexity theory is a deep
      and endlessly fascinating subject, regularly taking us the outer edges of what
      is knowable in the physical universe (and beyond!); if you want to know more, 
      there is perhaps no better place to start than Scott Aaronson's highly
      engaging book \emph{Quantum Computing Since Democritus}~\cite{Aaronson13}.
